- [[kvcache-cache-in-the-wild-characterizing-and-optimizing-kvcache-cache]] — KVCache Cache in the Wild: Characterizing and Optimizing KVCache Cache — Shanghai Jiao Tong University researchers with Alibaba Group engineers present the first systematic characterization of KV-cache (KV$) reuse patterns from production traces at Alibaba Cloud (ALIYUN), covering two representative workloads: Trace A (a to-C workload -- browser/mobile chatbot users) and Trace B (a to-B workload -- developers sending OpenAI-compatible API calls). KEY MEASURED FINDINGS: (1) Ideal (infinite-capacity) KV-cache hit rates are 62% (Trace A, to-C) and 54% (Trace B, to-B) -- lower than previously reported numbers on synthetic/ShareGPT-style datasets, meaning synthetic benchmarks overstate achievable cache benefit. (2) KV-cache reuse is highly skewed: 10% of KV-cache blocks contribute 77% of all reuses. (3) Contrary to the expectation that multi-turn conversations dominate cache reuse, single-turn requests account for 97% of KV-cache reuses in the to-B (API) workload -- single-turn reuse is NOT a negligible phenomenon and must be designed for, not just multi-turn session locality. (4) User-level reuse is also skewed: 19% of users in Trace A and just 4% of users in Trace B account for more than 90% of cache block hits, motivating the authors' recommendation to prioritize intra-user over inter-user KV-cache reuse in system design. (5) KV-cache lifespan is ephemeral: P99 lifespan of KV-cache entries in the to-B workload is 97 seconds, meaning a relatively small cache suffices -- specifically, cache capacity of about 2x per-GPU HBM is enough to approach the ideal infinite-capacity hit rate under common GQA configurations (for this workload). (6) Reuse-time and reuse-probability distributions vary by request category (API vs chat, and number of turns) but are predictable within a category from historical data, which the paper exploits to build a workload-aware cache eviction policy that outperforms generic (LRU-style) eviction, especially under limited cache capacity. `review`

NOTE ON EVIDENCE TYPE: this is a benchmark/algorithmic systems paper evaluated on synthetic workload traces and single-/multi-GPU testbeds (A10 GPUs cited for Fig.1), not a production-measurement paper -- it belongs to the systems layer (vLLM/SGLang/RadixAttention lineage) rather than the measured-production-evidence theme, but documents the fairness-vs-locality tradeoff that any multi-tenant internal LLM platform must resolve.

PROBLEM STATEMENT: existing fair-scheduling algorithms for LLM serving (e.g., Virtual Token Counter/VTC) ignore prefix locality and thus under-exploit prefix/KV-cache reuse, while existing locality-aware schedulers in production serving frameworks (SGLang's RadixAttention-based scheduler, Preble) maximize prefix-cache hit rate without any fairness guarantee -- meaning a dominant or adversarial client's requests can starve other tenants' latency by monopolizing cache-friendly routing.

MEASURED RESULTS: DLPM/D2LPM achieve up to 2.87x higher throughput than VTC (the only other fairness-guaranteeing algorithm) and up to 7.18x lower latency for 'victim' (well-behaved, non-dominant) clients compared to the state-of-the-art locality-aware-but-fairness-blind scheduler Preble; on average D2LPM achieves 2.90x lower latency than Preble and 4.06x lower latency than round-robin+LPM. Without a fairness guarantee, locality-maximizing schedulers like Preble and round-robin+LPM can inflict up to 7.18x and 9.55x higher latency respectively on well-behaved clients when a misbehaving/dominant client is present in the mix, because these schedulers preferentially route to maximize cache hit rate for whichever client already dominates cache state. DLPM provides rigorous theoretical fairness bounds (service bound and latency bound across client types) while preserving a tunable relaxation parameter Q that trades fairness strictness for throughput, achieving a new Pareto frontier of fairness (Jain Index) vs. throughput relative to plain LPM and VTC. `review`

- [[preble-efficient-distributed-prompt-scheduling-2]] — PREBLE: EFFICIENT DISTRIBUTED PROMPT SCHEDULING — UC San Diego's Preble paper (ICLR 2024, arXiv 2407.00023) is the primary systems-paper source for the locality-vs-load-balancing tension discussed throughout this batch. It identifies that prior KV-cache-reuse work (including SGLang/RadixAttention) is confined to single-GPU optimization, while production LLM serving is inherently distributed across many GPUs -- so prefix-cache reuse decisions must be co-optimized with cross-GPU load balancing, not treated as a single-instance problem. Preble's architecture is a two-level hierarchical scheduler: a global scheduler makes request-level placement decisions across GPU instances using a new 'E2' (Exploitation + Exploration) algorithm driven by a prompt-aware load metric (accounting for historical load, eviction cost, and the cost of serving a new request), while a per-instance local scheduler handles iteration-level scheduling of the requests assigned to it. Motivation grounded in real usage: notes that production prompts have grown long relative to generated output (prefill now dominates total compute) and that prompts are frequently partially shared across requests -- citing Anthropic's own public reporting of widespread long/shared prompts in production as evidence this is not a synthetic concern. Evaluated on real workloads and arrival patterns with two open-source LLMs, Preble outperforms prior state-of-the-art serving systems by 1.5x-14.5x on average latency and 2x-10x on p99 latency. This paper is the system that the later Locality-aware Fair Scheduling in LLM Serving paper (DLPM/D2LPM, same batch) explicitly targets as the locality-optimal-but-fairness-blind baseline, showing Preble can impose up to 7.18x higher latency on well-behaved clients when a dominant client is present -- i.e., Preble solves the locality/load-balance tension but not the fairness dimension. `review`
