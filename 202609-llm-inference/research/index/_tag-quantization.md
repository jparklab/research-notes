- [[awq-activation-aware-weight-quantization-for]] — AWQ: ACTIVATION-AWARE WEIGHT QUANTIZATION FOR — AWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization), Lin, Tang, Tang, et al., MIT/NVIDIA/MIT-IBM, MLSys 2024 Best Paper (arXiv:2306.00978v6). Proposes a post-training, weight-only low-bit (INT4/INT3) quantization method for LLMs that requires no backpropagation or reconstruction. Key insight: roughly 0.1-1% of weight channels are "salient" and disproportionately affect output quality; salience is identified by activation magnitude (not weight magnitude), and AWQ protects salient channels by per-channel scaling (scale weights up, scale corresponding activations down by the same factor) before quantizing everything uniformly to INT4/INT3 -- avoiding the hardware-inefficient mixed-precision kernels that a naive "keep salient weights in FP16" approach would require. Because it does no reconstruction against a calibration set, the paper argues AWQ generalizes better out-of-domain than GPTQ, whose Hessian-based reconstruction can overfit the calibration set.

Measured accuracy: on OPT-6.7B under INT3 with group size 128, naive round-to-nearest gives perplexity 43.2; keeping the top 1% salient weights in FP16 (mixed precision) drops this to 13.0; AWQ's scaling approach matches that 13.0 PPL without mixed precision. On Wikitext-2, AWQ beats both plain GPTQ and GPTQ-with-reordering on LLaMA and Llama-2 models at INT4-g128. On a multimodal benchmark (OpenFlamingo-9B on COCO captioning, 32-shot), quantization-induced degradation drops from 4.57 (baseline PTQ approaches) to 1.17 under INT4-g128 -- "4x model size reduction with negligible performance loss" is the paper's own characterization, not an independent audit.

System contribution: TinyChat, a 4-bit-native inference engine (on-device/edge-focused, built for Jetson Orin Nano, laptop/mobile GPUs, Raspberry Pi) using kernel fusion and platform-aware weight packing to turn AWQ's ~4x memory reduction into "3.2-3.3x average speedup" over the HuggingFace FP16 baseline across model families, and to make Llama-2-70B deployable on a single Jetson Orin (64GB). This is explicitly an edge/on-device framing, distinct from the data-center server-inference stacks (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM) that later adopted the AWQ format.

Ecosystem adoption claimed by the paper (2026 revision): AWQ is used by HuggingFace Transformers, NVIDIA TensorRT-LLM, Microsoft DirectML, Google Vertex AI, Intel Neural Compressor, Amazon SageMaker, AMD, FastChat, vLLM, and LMDeploy, and is credited with enabling Falcon-180B on a single H200 GPU. Core thesis: engines' built-in prefix caching only keeps KV cache inside GPU memory (vLLM's native CPU offloading does page-by-page transfer at 88 Gbps and lacks a distributed/hierarchical storage backend); LMCache instead extracts KV cache out of the engine entirely and treats it as a first-class, externally-managed data structure that can be moved across GPU, CPU DRAM, local/remote disk, S3, and network links (achieving 400 Gbps loading from CPU vs vLLM native's 88 Gbps), shared across queries AND across separate engine instances (cross-engine PD disaggregation transfer), with explicit control APIs (pin, lookup, clear, move, compress/decompress) that neither vLLM's nor SGLang's native caching exposes.

Technical mechanisms: (1) chunk-based batched I/O (default 256 tokens/chunk vs. vLLM's native 16-token pages) via a streaming GPU buffer to saturate interconnect bandwidth -- prior work cited shows transfers need to reach ~16MB to saturate 400Gbps NICs and 1-2MB to hit 75-80% of PCIe 5.0 bandwidth; (2) a standardized "KV connector" interface (jointly designed/maintained by the LMCache and vLLM teams, live in vLLM for over six months at publication) that decouples LMCache from inference-engine internals that change with ~15-20 new open-weight models released per week; (3) a centralized KV cache controller (manager plus per-instance workers) exposing lookup/move/pin/compress APIs for cache-aware routing and migration; (4) layer-wise pipelining and async prefetch to overlap compute with I/O; (5) zero-copy reference counting and a dynamic-offloading three-pointer scheme to avoid duplicating free GPU pages.

Adoption/integration: the paper states LMCache is used in -- and is the actual KV-caching implementation inside -- vLLM Production Stack, NVIDIA Dynamo, llm-d (Red Hat), and KServe; it also lists AIBrix (ByteDance) and SGLang/OME as peer Kubernetes-native inference stacks in the same category but does not claim LMCache is used inside AIBrix. It explicitly contrasts itself with proprietary prefix caching at Fireworks AI and Together AI (closed, not available to self-hosters) and with storage layers like Mooncake, Redis, InfiniStore, 3FS (lack an engine-integration "glue" layer or are tied to one framework). Over roughly a year, community contributions expanded backend support from 3 (local CPU, local disk, Redis) to 11 (adding NFS, WEKA, GPU-Direct Storage, Mooncake Store, NIXL, S3, InfiniStore, Valkey) across four processor types (NVIDIA, AMD, Ascend, TPU) and two engines (vLLM, SGLang).

Measured gains (vLLM v0.10.2 baseline, LMCache v0.3.6, 8xH100 unless noted): vs basic vLLM, basic vLLM CPU offloading, and two unnamed commercial inference APIs on synthetic multi-round QA (10-20K token contexts; Llama-3.1-8B/70B, Qwen2.5-72B/Coder-32B, Qwen3-Coder-480B-FP8) -- "1.9-8.1x smaller TTFT" and "2.3-14x higher throughput" at matched TTFT; one commercial baseline couldn't serve Qwen3-Coder-480B at all. On two real anonymized enterprise traces ("Company F", "Company G"): 3.7-6.8x smaller TTFT and 19-58% smaller ITL at high QPS. Centralized remote-storage sharing (15 Gbps link, TriviaQA/LongBench): 1.3-3x throughput improvement. PD disaggregation vs. vLLM's native NIXL-based PD transfer: 1.53-1.84x lower mean TTFT, 1.12-1.66x lower mean ITL, and markedly better tail latency, because LMCache transfers chunk-by-chunk into a GPU buffer rather than scattered page-by-page. A sensitivity study found KV-cache loading only beats fresh prefill when network bandwidth is low (32 Gbps) AND context exceeds 256K tokens; at 64-128 Gbps it wins at all context lengths -- a load-bearing caveat that offloading is not unconditionally better than recomputation.

- [[published-as-a-conference-paper-at-iclr-2023]] — Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2023 — GPTQ (Frantar, Ashkboos, Hoefler, Alistarh; IST Austria/ETH Zurich/NeuralMagic; ICLR 2023, arXiv:2210.17323v2). The baseline post-training weight quantization method that AWQ, llm-compressor, and every production quantization guide in this batch define themselves against. GPTQ is a one-shot, layer-wise weight quantization method based on approximate second-order (Hessian) information: it quantizes GPT-family models to 3- or 4-bit weights with claimed "negligible accuracy degradation" relative to FP16, more than doubling the compression-vs-accuracy tradeoff of prior round-to-nearest (RTN) methods at the time. Headline result: GPTQ quantizes OPT-175B or BLOOM-176B in approximately four GPU-hours, enabling OPT-175B to run for the first time on a single NVIDIA A100 (or two NVIDIA A6000 GPUs) instead of a multi-GPU cluster, with measured end-to-end inference speedups of approximately 3.25x on A100 and 4.5x on A6000 from custom GPU kernels that accelerate memory loading of the compressed weights. The paper is explicit about its own limitations, directly relevant to why AWQ and later methods were developed: GPTQ "currently does not provide speedups for the actual multiplications, due to the lack of hardware support for mixed-precision operands," and it does not quantize activations (weight-only, W4A16-style), leaving that to orthogonal techniques. This is the mechanism AWQ's authors cite when arguing GPTQ's Hessian-based reconstruction can overfit the calibration set -- GPTQ's own paper does not test or claim out-of-distribution generalization, which is the specific gap AWQ's non-reconstruction, activation-aware scaling approach was designed to close.
