# Rack power density
What you will learn 
GPU clusters consume 15–25 kW per rack; your data center's power budget and cooling infrastructure determine your maximum model size and training throughput before procurement and facility work becomes the critical path.

Why this matters
Power density directly limits the hardware you can deploy. A cluster design that looks viable on paper becomes impossible when the facility cannot deliver 400A three-phase power to a single rack or remove 25 kW of heat. Understanding power constraints before you design your training infrastructure prevents months of delay and vendor lock-in to suboptimal hardware choices. You'll also negotiate better pricing and timelines when you know the hard physical limits of your deployment environment.
## Explanation
Rack power density in GPU clusters is measured in kilowatts per 42U rack and is the primary physical constraint on ML infrastructure scaling. Modern H100 or H200 GPU nodes consume 700–900W per node; an 8-GPU DGX system draws 6.5–7.5 kW sustained, plus network and storage overhead. A fully loaded 42U rack with 4–5 such nodes reaches 25–30 kW, compared to 5–8 kW for typical CPU-only enterprise racks. **The challenge is not the GPU itself: it is the data center infrastructure to support it.** Most enterprise facilities are provisioned for 4–6 kW per rack (legacy standard from 2010–2015). Upgrading to 25+ kW per rack requires new power distribution (often 400A or higher three-phase feeds per rack), enhanced cooling (in-row or rear-door heat exchangers instead of room-level CRAC units), and raised floor airflow redesign. Even cloud providers with purpose-built GPU clusters must place power-dense racks in specific zones with dedicated infrastructure. On-premises deployments typically require facility upgrades costing $50k–$200k per rack and 4–12 weeks of lead time for power and cooling work. Colocation and edge facilities often cannot support 25 kW per rack without custom arrangements. This is why most organizations start with cloud GPU offerings (AWS, GCP, Azure) where power density is pre-engineered, then move to on-premises only after validating model and training workflow.
## Reality check
What works
  * Cloud providers (AWS p4d, GCP a3-megagpu, Azure NDv5) have solved power density and can scale to 100+ petaFLOPs for a single cluster
  * Colocation facilities with GPU-ready cages (Equinix, Digital Realty) can provision 15–25 kW per rack in 2–4 weeks if you contract in advance
  * Shared on-premises GPU clusters (not per-team, but centrally managed) amortize infrastructure cost and power overhead across multiple training jobs
  * Power-efficient model designs (quantization, pruning, knowledge distillation) reduce inference power by 40–60%, allowing smaller racks to scale


What doesn't
  * You cannot retrofit standard enterprise data centers to 25 kW per rack without $100k+ in power and cooling work per rack: the wiring, breakers, and HVAC are simply not there
  * Liquid cooling is not a magic solution for on-premises: it requires specialized plumbing, maintenance expertise, and compatibility with existing facility systems. Most enterprises are not prepared for it
  * You cannot overpack a single rack beyond physical limits; 8 H100 nodes in 42U is the practical maximum, not 10 or 12
  * Power budgets are not fungible: a 20 kW per-rack budget does not mean you can run two 10 kW racks in series if your facility has only one 30A circuit available


Timeline reality
Cloud GPU provisioning: same-day to 1 week. On-premises power and cooling upgrade: 6–12 weeks from design to go-live. Colocation with existing power: 2–4 weeks. New facility build: 6–12 months.
THE ONE TRUTH
Power and cooling are purchased 12–18 months before compute. If you do not reserve capacity early, you will either pay 3–5x premium for expedited installation or defer your cluster by a release cycle.
## Compliance note
Regulation NFPA 110 (electrical safety), IEC 62040 (UPS systems), local building codes (electrical load and fire safety)
What it means for engineers
Your facility design must be reviewed by a licensed electrical engineer and approved by the local building/fire authority before GPU racks are deployed. You cannot self-certify power distribution. UPS capacity must support graceful shutdown (typically 5–15 minutes) of the entire cluster; undersized UPS causes data loss and corrupted checkpoints. Backup power for cooling is often neglected: a data center without HVAC fails in 20–30 minutes during a power event.
Vendor implications
Major colocation providers (Equinix, Digital Realty) pre-certify their infrastructure for 25+ kW per rack and handle compliance. Cloud providers (AWS, GCP) certify their GPU regions. On-premises deployment requires hiring a facilities consulting firm ($10k–$30k) and coordinating with your IT/facilities team to validate local electrical codes.
Compliance gotcha
Engineers design a 100-GPU cluster assuming continuous 15 kW per rack, but peak power draw (all GPUs under load, all fans at max) is 20–22 kW. If UPS and PDU (power distribution unit) are sized for average power, the breaker will trip during a full training run. Always size for peak, not average.
##  Phased GPU cluster deployment with cloud-first pilot and on-premises buildout 
  1. Phase 0 (Month 1): Run training jobs on cloud GPU (AWS p4d or GCP a3). Measure actual power draw, networking, and storage I/O requirements. Do not assume vendor specs: measure.
  2. Phase 1 (Month 2–3): Conduct facility audit. Document existing power distribution, UPS capacity, cooling, and network. Identify gaps for 25+ kW per rack.
  3. Phase 2 (Month 4–6): If on-premises is viable, engage electrical engineer and facilities team. Design power (feeder sizing, PDU configuration), cooling (in-row units, tile-level airflow), and network (10GbE or 400GbE spine to reduce congestion). Establish procurement lead times.
  4. Phase 3 (Month 7–12): Install and stress-test. Run continuous workloads and monitor power stability, temperature, and network performance. Validate checkpoint I/O to storage.
  5. Phase 4 (Ongoing): Meter power per rack and per job. Implement power capping in Slurm or Kubernetes to prevent brownouts. Track cost per GPU-hour.


Critical step
Phase 1 facility audit is routinely skipped. Teams assume the data center can handle 25 kW per rack because it was upgraded 'recently.' In reality, recent upgrades often mean 8–10 kW per rack, not 25. Audits reveal that your facility has no spare power capacity, and a new feeder requires 8–16 weeks of coordination with your power utility. Do this audit before purchasing hardware.
Human in the loop
Facilities manager or electrical engineer must sign off on power budget before racks are ordered. Cooling engineer must validate HVAC strategy during design. Network engineer must ensure switch uplink capacity is not saturated. After deployment, an on-call person must monitor UPS and facility alerts; GPU clusters without facility oversight will lose jobs to power events.
## Tool recommendations
Primary stack
Slurm or Kubernetes with NVIDIA device plugin for job scheduling; Prometheus + Grafana for power and temperature monitoring per rack; Smart PDUs (e.g., Vertiv, Eaton) to meter power per circuit; thermal imaging during commissioning to identify hot spots; gpustat or nvidia-smi logging to correlate GPU load with facility power draw.
Why not direct API
Not applicable: this is infrastructure, not an API question. However, relying solely on cloud GPUs to avoid power provisioning is a common trap. Once your cluster exceeds $500k/month in cloud spend, on-premises becomes cost-effective, but only if power and cooling are amortized over 3–5 years.
Cost estimate
Cloud GPU pilot (8 GPUs, 3 months): $8k–$15k. On-premises facility upgrade (25 kW per rack, new power feeder, HVAC, PDU, UPS): $50k–$200k per rack (one-time). Ongoing: $0.08–$0.15 per kWh for electricity + ~$2k/month for colocation or ~$1k/month facilities maintenance for on-premises.
Real-world failure
Hypothetical scenario based on documented industry patterns: A mid-sized AI company commissioned a 64-GPU on-premises cluster (8 racks) without conducting a facility audit. Design assumed 20 kW per rack. After installation, the first full training run triggered facility breakers every 30–40 minutes. Root cause: the new power feeder was undersized; it could deliver only 15 kW sustained per rack before protective relays opened the circuit. The facility upgrade took 6 weeks, during which all GPU racks were offline. The delay pushed the model release by a quarter and cost $2M in lost revenue. The on-premises investment that was supposed to reduce cloud spend became more expensive than cloud would have been.
What went wrong
Infrastructure design and facility planning were decoupled. The ML team designed the cluster, but no electrical or facilities engineer was consulted until hardware arrived. By then, retrofitting the facility was the only option.
Lesson
Engage electrical and facilities engineers during cluster design, not after procurement. Do a facility audit first. Cost of an audit ($5k–$15k) is trivial compared to delay costs. Always measure actual power draw on a small pilot before designing the full cluster.
Stakeholder note
Engage the facilities/operations manager and electrical engineer at the start of cluster planning, not at the end. The common mistake: ML engineers and procurement finalize hardware purchases, then tell facilities, 'We need 25 kW per rack by next month.' Facilities cannot deliver in that timeline. Instead, involve them in Phase 0: ask them what power density the facility supports today, what lead time is required to upgrade, and what the cost is. Let that data drive your hardware choices. For cloud deployments, engage cloud account managers early to understand instance availability and regional capacity in your target zones.
MVP path
Deploy a 2-rack pilot (8–10 GPUs) on cloud or colocation with existing power. Run your standard training workloads for 2 weeks and measure: average and peak power per rack, network throughput to storage, checkpoint I/O latency, and cost per GPU-hour. Document the power profile and present it to your facilities team with a simple ask: 'Can you provision this power and cooling for 8 more racks?' If yes, you have a roadmap. If no, you know you need cloud or a different facility. This pilot costs $5k–$10k and saves $500k+ in wrong decisions.
Procurement reality
If you are procuring on-premises infrastructure: facility audit takes 2–3 weeks, electrical design takes 4–6 weeks, and procurement/installation of power and cooling takes 8–12 weeks. Total: 4–5 months before the first rack can be powered on. Cloud GPU procurement: 1–2 weeks, contingent on account setup and region availability. If your timeline is tight, start with cloud and migrate on-premises after validation.
Experienced dev note
The engineers who avoid power density disasters are those who involve a facilities engineer in month 1, not month 6. You will find that your facility's power density limit is lower than you assumed, and that upgrade timelines are much longer. This discovery early lets you right-size your cluster to the facility or commit to cloud sooner. The cost of a facilities audit ($5k–$15k) and a brief design review with an electrical engineer is nothing compared to the cost of a 3-month delay or a $100k+ emergency facility upgrade. Also: measure power before you buy hardware. A vendor's thermal design power (TDP) is not the same as actual sustained power draw. Test on a small pilot first.

This is technical guidance on infrastructure design, not legal or regulatory advice. Consult a licensed electrical engineer and your local building authority before designing high-density power systems. Compliance with NFPA 110 and local electrical codes is mandatory.
