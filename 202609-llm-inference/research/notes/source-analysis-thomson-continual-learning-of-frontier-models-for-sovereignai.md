# Source Analysis — Thomson: Continual Learning of Frontier Models for SovereignAI

**Original source:** [[thomson-continual-learning-of-frontier-models-for]]
**Source type:** paper (arXiv technical report)
**Source word count:** 66,217
**Your judgment:** The single most important source in the corpus for "what to build vs. adopt" — a first-person, named-component account of Thomson Reuters' internal model-and-serving programme. It is comparatively thin on serving-engine specifics (one subsection, ~1,400 words, out of 66K) but what it does say about serving is precise, dated, and quantified, and the paper is explicit that the self-hosted vLLM-based platform is a production system already carrying real internal + customer traffic, not a research prototype.

*Suggested by [[thomson-continual-learning-of-frontier-models-for]] — source analyst's digest of the full source body*

## Thesis / Central claim
Frontier-level model performance is achievable by non-hyperscaler institutions via "Continual Learning" (continued pre-training / "mid-training" + value realignment + post-training) on top of open-weight checkpoints, rather than pretraining from scratch or shallow fine-tuning — and the same sovereignty logic that justifies owning the model extends to owning the serving/inference stack rather than depending on a frontier vendor's API. The report frames itself explicitly as a "model factory" blueprint, not just a model release: "the two sets of results describe a model factory: a reproducible pipeline for turning an open-weight checkpoint and an institution's proprietary data into a deployable, competitive, and governable system."

## Methodology / Basis of claims
First-person engineering/research report (not a benchmark paper or survey) by Thomson Reuters researchers with Imperial College London, DatologyAI, and Lambda as named partners. Claims are supported by: (a) internal training telemetry (step-time breakdowns, GPU-hour accounting, throughput deltas between colocation strategies); (b) a custom evaluation suite (CapTrack + a broader test-eval suite) run across intermediate checkpoints; (c) operational telemetry from a live production serving system (request counts, token counts, success rates, egress-cache hit rates measured Feb–Aug 2026); (d) a blind human preference study (>3,000 expert-rated conversations) comparing the resulting model, given access to internal tool/data sources, against OpenAI/Anthropic models given web access. Load-bearing assumption throughout: that improvements measured via their own CapTrack/benchmark harness and vals.ai-sourced cost baselines generalize to real-world deployment, and that third-party model costs (estimated from "publicly available pricing data from Alibaba," accessed August 2026) are comparable apples-to-apples with their own measured GPU costs.

## Key findings / Claims (with specific numbers where present)

1. **Base models and resulting checkpoints.** Thomson-1.0-Small (35B) is built from Qwen3.6-35B; Thomson-1.0-Large is built from Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (a large MoE). A separate, Imperial-College-codeveloped value-realigned variant of the same Qwen bases is called Snowdon-1.0-Large / Snowdon-1.1-Small, produced through "the Frontier AI Research Lab, a joint research lab established by Thomson Reuters and Imperial College London."

2. **Training-side cost figures (training, not inference — flagged as contextual only per scope).** "The cost of the final training run for Thomson-1.0-Large (measured in GPU costs over three weeks of training) is conservatively estimated to be under USD 450,000. The total cost of development (including staff, compute costs, domain expert compensation, and vendor partnerships) is estimated at approximately USD 40M, with most of it dedicated to reusable research, infrastructure engineering & experimentation over a meaningfully longer time period than the aforementioned three months." Team size: "not exceeding three dozen engineers and scientists," on "no more than 368 B200 GPUs available at any stage of experimentation" (hardware figure cited only because it is inseparable from the cost claim per the scope constraint). Total development timeline: three months from first Qwen3.5-397B experiments.

3. **The post-training pipeline is three sequential modules**, each producing a usable Foundation Model checkpoint: (i) Value focus — constitutional DPO (+ optional activation steering) on the open-weight instruction-tuned base; (ii) Knowledge focus — data-centric "mid-training"/continued pre-training (CPT) on curated proprietary + synthetic + replay data, followed by model merging to protect general capability; (iii) Behaviour/skill/tool focus — two-stage DPO (general preference, then agentic) warm-starting two-stage RL (short-context general, then long-context end-to-end Deep Research). SFT as an isolated stage is explicitly avoided: "we explicitly forgo any further supervised fine-tuning (SFT) as an isolated post-training stage due to its tendency to aggressively alter model behaviour, leading to rapid forgetting even across broad data distributions."

4. **Training/RL framework stack, named explicitly.** "Our training library extends Nvidia NeMo-RL, with Megatron-LM core to provide model parallelism and vLLM for generation. NeMo-RL implements distributed worker orchestration via Ray, the GRPO and DPO algorithms, and checkpoint tooling based on PyTorch Distributed. On top of this we implement 28 custom extensions together with custom NeMo-Gym RL task environments and a collection of specialised reward functions." So: NVIDIA NeMo-RL (open source, forked/extended) + Megatron-LM (model parallelism) + Ray (distributed orchestration) + vLLM (used here as the RL *rollout/generation* engine, not yet the production serving engine) + PyTorch Distributed (checkpointing). All open source, all modified in-house with 28 named custom extensions (zero-learning-signal fixes, policy-gradient loss variants, reference-model-removal memory optimization, cohort reward reconciliation for judge failures).

5. **Data curation: built on an external vendor partnership, not built in-house.** DatologyAI is a named commercial partner, not an OSS project: "we partnered with DatologyAI to curate a high-quality mid-training dataset of 200B tokens from a corpus of permissively public and high-quality proprietary data of over 19T tokens" — i.e., 98%+ of the candidate pool was pruned. Replay data is "DatologyAI's general-capability mid-training mixture"; synthetic data uses "an adaptation of DatologyAI's BeyondWeb method." Mid-training recipe (filters, mixture ratios) was validated using small open proxy models (Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.1-8B) and open corpora before being applied to the real proprietary corpus — explicitly to avoid any eval/proprietary-data leakage into methodology development.

6. **CapTrack — the single most clearly bespoke-and-justified tool in the paper.** A custom capability-tracking meta-benchmark built specifically because no existing benchmark gave "a multifaceted view of model quality beyond aggregate accuracy." It is explicitly not a new benchmark but a re-organization of community benchmarks along three axes — CAN (latent competence), WILL (default behavioural preferences), HOW (protocol compliance/execution) — paired with a semantic-deduplication/subsampling technique the authors call "Scales++," which reduces each benchmark to ~10–15% of its original examples while preserving signal, making repeated evaluation across many checkpoints computationally tractable. Published separately as companion papers ([11] CapTrack, [13] Scales++).

7. **Inference/serving infrastructure — the directly relevant section for the research query (§5.1.2, §5.4), and notably short relative to the paper's length.** "Developing this in-house has enabled upfront cost reduction in reserved instances. Additionally, a large number of contemporary models are still unavailable via enterprise options. In such cases, self-hosting allows us to include these models in our benchmarking efforts... Self-hosting allows our team a high degree of flexibility in hosting proprietary and internal models." The engine is vLLM, wrapped in an in-house-built orchestration/routing layer: "Our LLM orchestration and inference platform is built on Kubernetes, with a routing, authorisation, and rate-limiting plane implemented in Rust, using vLLM as an inference backend. It operates over 11 independent clusters spread across 4 geographies and 2 cloud providers," with routing clusters and inference clusters kept strictly separate for blast-radius containment. The platform exposes an API "mirroring OpenAI's Responses API."

8. **Production serving traffic figures, dated and precise.** "Since April 2026, this system has served more than 40M requests and processed 370B tokens, with upwards of a 99.9% success rate, with cumulative use across the broader organisation reaching 146M requests and nearly 700B tokens in that same period. In the case of GLM-5.2, self-hosting has saved 19.7% of costs, compared to third-party inference providers." This is the clearest inference-side economics data point in the source — a measured 19.7% self-hosting saving on a specific named open-weight model (GLM-5.2) versus third-party inference providers, and real production volume (40M requests / 370B tokens on the model-development team's own usage; 146M requests / ~700B tokens org-wide) since a dated start (April 2026).

9. **vLLM customizations made in-house, stated and scoped.** "We customise vLLM to support novel models and features prior to availability of longer-term support in the vLLM upstream. We introduced the ability to define a thinking budget client-side by introducing custom logits processors, and implemented custom reasoning parsers to support models not otherwise natively capable of reasoning traces." Two specific optimizations are named as yielding "the largest benefit": (a) Server Initialisation — shifting model weights to an in-memory volume plus a "faster-loading library (e.g., instanttensor)" cut model cold-start from "often 20 minutes or longer" to "approximately five minutes, and often less," explicitly to avoid cascading-failure risk from slow replica starts; (b) Quantisation — dynamic post-training FP8 quantization via vLLM's own `llm-compressor` library (not a custom quantizer), with `lm_head` and MoE gating/routing layers deliberately left unquantized to avoid quality loss.

10. **Data infrastructure for the GPU training cluster is built on two named open-source foundations, fully reused for serving-adjacent data movement too**: DataHub (artifact/URN catalogue, "source of truth for the indirection layer") and SeaweedFS (in-cluster artifact cache). Stated reason for building the indirection layer in-house atop these OSS pieces: cross-cloud egress costs are "prohibitive on both latency and cost grounds," and the measured counterfactual savings were "over USD 1.4M in avoided egress costs" between February and August 2026 (16.13 PB of read demand against only 278.7 TB actually egressed, a 57.9x reduction) — a training/data-infra cost, not an inference cost, but illustrative of the pattern of "take OSS building blocks, wrap bespoke orchestration/caching logic around them."

11. **Job orchestration is a modified fork of an existing OSS platform, not built from scratch.** "Job submission, scheduling, and experiment tracking are handled by a substantially modified fork of the open-source ClearML platform, extended to accommodate the heterogeneity of our execution environments," paired with an internally-built job submission/inspection UI layer — explicitly the mechanism that "is what makes relocation tractable" across GPU providers/regions.

12. **Deep Research agent harness is an adapted open-source project, used for training/evaluation (not stated to be the production customer-facing path).** "We adapt DeerFlow, an open-source LangGraph research agent implementing the orchestrator-worker pattern... We retain the harness topology, with significant adaptations to the evaluation and training to address unique challenges in non-verifiable domains." This is a training/eval-time agent scaffold (planner → isolated ReAct researcher workers → fan-in/re-plan → tool-less reporter), built to exploit private/licensed data sources (Reuters News archive, legal databases) rather than open web search — directly supporting the "data sovereignty" argument.

13. **Explicit rejection of distillation and of parameter-efficient fine-tuning as primary methods**, stated as a design principle rather than an afterthought: "We make full-weight updates (rather than relying on parameter-efficient methods) and do not engage in large-scale distillation, which both assumes the existence of a stronger teacher model and is typically unable to surpass it."

14. **Sovereignty is explicitly framed as a spectrum, not a binary, and infrastructure/serving ownership is one of five pillars.** The paper enumerates five "essential principles of SovereignAI": (§1) Training & Model Sovereignty, (§2) Data & Tool Sovereignty, (§3) Governance & Ethical Alignment, (§4) Infrastructure Sovereignty ("Largely achievable by building on increasingly mature open-source stacks for training, model serving & tool infrastructure. Remaining dependence on critical hardware infrastructure."), (§5) Control Over Economic Factors ("Substantially increased by operating models at cost while maintaining control over release/update cycles. Remaining uncertainty based on cost/access to serving hardware.").

15. **Headline benchmark result (secondary to the research query but load-bearing for "did this programme succeed"):** Thomson-1.0-Large is reported to match or surpass Claude Sonnet 5, GLM-5.2, GPT-5.5, DeepSeek V4 Pro, and Gemini 3.1 Pro on a range of legal/tax/multilingual/Deep Research/agentic tasks, with a "π-shaped" improvement pattern: broad capability gains, not just in the targeted domain, with minimal catastrophic forgetting (coding is the one area showing mild regression relative to the open-weight base).

## Caveats, limitations, contradictions
- **The paper itself flags that closed-model comparisons cannot fully control for scaffolding**: "closed models are accessible only as served endpoints, and we cannot rule out provider-side routing, system prompt augmentation or other scaffolding. We consider these unavoidable rather than disqualifying." This weakens the strength of head-to-head performance claims against Sonnet 5 / GPT-5.5 / Gemini 3.1 Pro, though it does not affect the engineering/stack claims this digest is built around.
- **Self-acknowledged incomplete sovereignty**: "the dependence on accelerator hardware is not removed" — infrastructure sovereignty is explicitly partial, and the authors frame their own GPU-cluster relocation strategy as a mitigation rather than a solution.
- **Coding capability regresses mildly** relative to the open-weight base — the one domain showing forgetting, acknowledged directly rather than hidden.
- **Serving section is strikingly short relative to overall length (~1,400 words out of 66,217, under 2.5% of the document) despite the system carrying real, large-scale production traffic.** This is itself a finding: a 66K-word paper devoted almost entirely to model training methodology spends disproportionately little space on how the resulting models are actually served. No latency SLOs, no P50/P99 figures, no concurrent-request/QPS figures, and no explicit statement of max context length served or batching strategy are given. Disaggregated prefill/decode, continuous batching, or KV-cache-specific optimizations (beyond the general mention of "trade KV cache locality for what would otherwise be a slower or outright failed request" under cross-region failover) are not discussed at all — this absence should be recorded as an audited absence rather than inferred.
- **No breakdown of the $40M total programme cost into training vs. inference/serving infrastructure spend** is given; the figure is explicitly staff+compute+domain-expert+vendor-partnership costs pooled together over "a meaningfully longer time period" than the three-month training window, so it cannot be used as an inference-specific cost figure.
- **The 19.7% self-hosting saving is reported for exactly one model (GLM-5.2)** and is not generalized to Thomson's own models or to the rest of the self-hosted model catalogue; readers should not extrapolate this percentage to other models without flagging the single-model provenance.
- **Potential conflict of interest / promotional framing**: DatologyAI and NVIDIA/Lambda components are described favorably throughout by a report co-authored with DatologyAI itself ("Co-authored by DatologyAI" appears at the mid-training data section), so claims about DatologyAI's BeyondWeb/replay-mixture efficacy should be read as partially self-interested vendor validation rather than fully independent evaluation.

## Relevance to research_query
This source is **load-bearing** for the research query's three central questions. On "what tech stack do they use": it names a full, specific stack across training (NeMo-RL + Megatron-LM + Ray + PyTorch Distributed), data infra (DataHub + SeaweedFS), job orchestration (a modified ClearML fork), and — most relevant — serving (vLLM on Kubernetes with a Rust-built routing/auth/rate-limiting plane across 11 clusters in 4 geographies). On "where they leverage open source, and which": nearly every infrastructure layer is built on a named OSS project (vLLM, NeMo-RL, Megatron-LM, Ray, ClearML, DataHub, SeaweedFS, LangGraph/DeerFlow, Inspect, NeMo-Gym), which directly answers the "which open source solutions" sub-question with proper names and versions/dates. On "where they build custom": the report is unusually explicit about naming 28 custom NeMo-RL extensions, a bespoke Rust routing/auth plane in front of vLLM, custom vLLM logits processors/reasoning parsers for models lacking native reasoning-trace support, a bespoke artifact-indirection layer (URN-based) stitched across DataHub/SeaweedFS, and two entirely bespoke evaluation tools (CapTrack, Scales++) — each with a stated reason (cost control, vendor lock-in avoidance, model availability gaps, uptime risk from slow cold starts, cross-cloud egress cost). The one gap: the research query is specifically about *inference*, and this source — while unusually forthcoming for a model-training paper — still devotes under 3% of its length to the serving layer itself, giving only one data point each on cost savings (19.7%, one model) and production scale (40M requests / 370B tokens since April 2026), with no latency/throughput/batching figures at all. This absence should be reported to the orchestrator as a gap worth chasing in a dedicated depth investigation (e.g., is there a separate Thomson Reuters engineering blog post or conference talk specifically on the serving platform, given how clearly it is described as a mature, multi-cluster, Rust-fronted vLLM deployment already carrying nine-digit request volumes?).

Stated rationale for building the inference/serving platform rather than buying it.

> "We customise vLLM to support novel models and features prior to availability of longer-term support in the vLLM upstream."
Direct statement that vLLM customization is driven by upstream feature lag, not dissatisfaction with vLLM as a base.

> "Our training library extends Nvidia NeMo-RL, with Megatron-LM core to provide model parallelism and vLLM for generation... On top of this we implement 28 custom extensions together with custom NeMo-Gym RL task environments and a collection of specialised reward functions."
Full named training stack plus the explicit count of custom extensions.

Clear example of fork-and-modify rather than build-from-scratch for orchestration tooling.

> "A critical reader may pause and consider the reliance on an initial open-weight starting point as incompatible with the principles of SovereignAI... we are interested in moving beyond a bipolar view of sovereignty... by instead advancing along what we consider a sovereignty spectrum."
The central justification for why a frontier-API-free, open-weight-based build was chosen over simply consuming a frontier API, framed as partial rather than absolute sovereignty.

> "We make full-weight updates (rather than relying on parameter-efficient methods) and do not engage in large-scale distillation, which both assumes the existence of a stronger teacher model and is typically unable to surpass it."
Explicit methodological rejection of two common cheaper alternatives (LoRA-style PEFT, distillation).

> "...the intelligent use and assembly of an increasingly mature open-source landscape can enable infrastructure sovereignty (§4)."
The paper's own closing framing of its infrastructure strategy as OSS-assembly rather than from-scratch engineering.
