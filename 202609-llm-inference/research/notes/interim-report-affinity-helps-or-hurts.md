# Interim report: affinity-helps-or-hurts

**Locus question:** Does routing requests to the pod holding their prefix cache reliably improve serving, or does affinity concentrate load on hot-prefix replicas and cut capacity below plain load balancing?
**Flavor:** dialectical

## What the corpus already said

The width sweep produced two apparently opposed bodies of evidence. On the "helps" side, [[accelerate-multi-turn-llm-workloads-on-openshift-ai-with-llm-d-intelligent-routi]] (Red Hat Developer, Jan 2026) reports a hands-on 4×L4 GPU benchmark where llm-d's prefix-aware routing cut P95 TTFT from 727ms to 237-272ms (63% faster) and raised KV cache hit rate from ~62% to ~90% versus Kubernetes round-robin, explicitly explaining the mechanism as "ensuring follow-up turns land on replicas that already contain the relevant context." On the "hurts" side, [[cacheroute-planned-prefix-affinity-routingfor-large-scale-llm-serving]] (Meta, arXiv 2608.19677) — already digested in [[source-analysis-cacheroute-planned-prefix-affinity-routing]] — reports that on two 32B semi-synthetic aggregate workloads, enabling prefix-affinity routing *reduces* capacity to 0.50-0.67× flat load balancing because the affinity hit-rate gain (1.1%→11.8% and 0.8%→8.5%) was too small to offset the residual load skew from pinning keys to destinations, and that in a controlled 8B ablation, affinity-only routing pushes load imbalance to 3.46× while capacity stays flat versus Flat-LB, only improving once LPT-style balanced placement is added (imbalance 1.24×, capacity hits the sweep ceiling). [[sticky-until-saturated-token-aware-routing-in-llm-d-llm-d]] (already digested in [[source-analysis-sticky-until-saturated-token-aware-routing-in-llm-d]]) is llm-d's own maintainer writeup of the *current* default router design, and it implicitly concedes CacheRoute's concern: it explicitly names "the hot spotting most routers suffer from over-indexing on KV-cache affinity... without an explicit saturation release the warm endpoint keeps absorbing load past the point where a cold endpoint would serve the request faster" as one of two failure modes motivating a router redesign. The loci-analyst flagged that no corpus note had yet connected llm-d's specific gate mechanics (filter threshold, saturation override, calibration formula) to CacheRoute's specific negative-result regime.

## What the new sources say

**[[sticky-until-saturated-token-aware-routing-in-llm-d-llm-d]] — full mechanism read.** This is llm-d's authoritative engineering spec for the current default scheduler, published alongside llm-d 0.9. The mechanism has two parts. (1) A **filter**: `prefix-cache-affinity-filter` computes an affinity score per endpoint (fraction of incoming prompt tokens already cached there); endpoints with affinity ≥ `affinityThreshold` (0.8 in the benchmarked setup) form a restricted candidate set — "if no endpoint meets the threshold, all endpoints pass through and the downstream scorer operates on the full set." (2) A **load-matched scorer** operating only within that restricted set: `token-load-scorer` for prefill-bound traffic (routes on uncached prefill tokens in flight, using a lookahead formula `score(endpoint) = current_uncached_in_flight(endpoint) + prompt_tokens × (1 - hit_rate(endpoint))`) or `active-request-scorer` for decode-bound traffic (routes on active stream count). The **saturation override** is the release valve: on the current llm-d main branch this is unified into "a single time-domain gate... stickiness breaks when the best warm endpoint's estimated TTFT exceeds the best non-warm endpoint's by more than `maxTTFTPenaltyMs`," with TTFT estimated from a calibrated `peakPrefillThroughput` (R_peak). The threshold is derived in closed form, not tuned empirically: `τ_sat = R_peak × T_max`, reference value 286,720 (Qwen3-32B/H100, T_max=14s) = "35 × 8192, or 35 max-num-batched-tokens chunks of pending uncached prefill work." The paper is explicit that this formula does not transplant across (model, accelerator, engine): peak prefill throughput ranges from 15,360 tok/s (Qwen3-32B/H100/SGLang) to 39,065 tok/s (gpt-oss-120B/H100/vLLM, a sparse MoE) in their measured table, a ~2.5× spread on the same accelerator family, so τ must be recalibrated per deployment.

Crucially, the b2b-saas benchmark (150 fixed prompts × 5 variants, deliberately engineered so aggregate cacheable content exceeds aggregate per-endpoint KV capacity — i.e., a small-scale analog of CacheRoute's skewed-key scenario) shows the gate **firing empirically exactly where the concentration risk becomes real**: "At very high QPS (50+) prefix+token's hit rate drops to 70–73% while the legacy blend holds at 96–97%; this reflects the τ=286720 saturation valve engaging on prefix+token when warm endpoints accumulate enough uncached in-flight tokens to trigger the override, briefly redistributing traffic away from warm endpoints. The legacy blend has no such valve." This is direct evidence the mechanism does what it claims: sacrifice some hit rate to prevent hot-replica overload once load, not just prefix state, signals danger.

**[[published-as-a-conference-paper-at-iclr-2026]] (DualMap, Huawei/HUST, ICLR 2026)** corroborates the "hurts" side independently of CacheRoute, on real Mooncake production traces (Qwen2.5-7B/14B, Ascend NPU): "pure cache-affinity routing (e.g., consistent hashing on prefix) causes severe load imbalance under skewed prompt popularity, overloading nodes serving hot prefixes while cold-prefix nodes idle, driving TTFT SLO violations," with "Cache Affinity alone collapsing under skewed Tool&Agent prefixes due to load imbalance." DualMap's fix — two-candidate hashing (power-of-two-choices) plus an SLO-aware switch to load-only routing once predicted TTFT exceeds budget, plus hotspot-aware migration — is structurally close to llm-d's filter+scorer+saturation-override pattern: both couple a locality signal with a load-triggered escape hatch rather than shipping pinned/pure affinity.

**[[tokenlake-a-unified-segment-level-prefix-cache-pool]] (Peking University/StepFun)** frames the same failure mode at the architectural level, independent of the specific router policy: "For instances with high hit rates, the memory bandwidth is over-utilized, whereas for instances with low hit rates, the memory bandwidth is under-utilized, leading to the load imbalance." TokenLake's diagnosis is that *any* instance-local, whole-request routing scheme (its examples: "systems like llm-d's KV-cache-aware router, SGLang, Mooncake") has this failure mode baked into the unit of granularity — a full prefix must sit on one instance to form a contiguous prefix tree — and its fix (segment-level cache pooling, decoupling cache placement from scheduling) is a different layer of the stack than llm-d's request-routing gate; it does not evaluate llm-d's saturation-release mechanism specifically and is not evidence that llm-d's gate fails, only that the underlying instance-local architecture has a structural ceiling the gate manages rather than eliminates.

**Verification of shipped defaults against primary GitHub source (not just the blog post).** A targeted fetch confirms, from the actual Go source and the current guide README rather than the benchmark-era blog parameterization, that this design is the live shipped default. [[prefixcacheaffinity-package-githubcomllm-dllm-d-routerpkgeppframeworkpluginssche]] (Godoc for `github.com/llm-d/llm-d-router/pkg/epp/framework/plugins/scheduling/filter/prefixcacheaffinity`, v0.10.0) documents the exported `DefaultConfig` struct verbatim: `AffinityThreshold=0.80`, `ExplorationProbability=0`, `MaxTTFTPenaltyMs=18000`, `TTFTSource=prefillThroughput`, `PeakPrefillThroughput=15928` tokens/sec — matching the blog's reconciled reference value (15,928 × 18s ≈ 286,704 ≈ the 286,720 derivation) to within rounding. [[llm-dguidesoptimized-baselinereadmemd-at-main-llm-dllm-d-github]] (GitHub README, commit 965a508, Sep 12 2026) confirms this is not a benchmark artifact but the operator-facing recommended default: "The optimized-baseline defaults to two main routing criteria: Prefix-cache aware using the prefix cache affinity filter... Load-aware using the token load scorer... Both plugins are used with their built-in defaults — no per-deployment tuning is required for this guide's reference setup." [[optimized-baseline-llm-d-2]] (llm-d.ai docs, dev branch) adds that this is explicitly framed as "one approach" — the router also ships session-affinity and active-request-only scorers as alternate, operator-selectable configurations — and flags the same calibration trap the blog raises: `peakPrefillThroughput=15928` is measured for one specific (model, accelerator) pair and silently under- or over-fires the saturation gate if transplanted to different hardware without recalibration. This closes the locus's main verification gap: the mechanism described above is confirmed current shipped behavior (v0.10.0, Sep 2026), not a superseded benchmark-era proposal.

## Evidence synthesis

### Position A: Cache-affinity routing reliably improves serving

The strongest version of this case is not "affinity always helps" but "affinity helps whenever a workload has enough recoverable prefix reuse relative to fleet size and skew, and llm-d's specific implementation is built to detect and exploit exactly that condition." The vendor benchmark ([[accelerate-multi-turn-llm-workloads-on-openshift-ai-with-llm-d-intelligent-routi]]) demonstrates large, consistent gains (P95 TTFT 727→237ms, hit rate 62%→90%) on a workload with genuine multi-turn locality (conversation continuity across turns) at small scale (4 replicas). CacheRoute's own 70B flagship result — the single most rigorous large-scale measurement in the corpus (Llama-3.3-70B, 60 H100 GPUs, 5 paired seeds) — shows the *same* qualitative pattern at far larger scale: 93.2% served KV hit vs. 64.1% for flat balancing, and 2.3× the SLO capacity of the best baseline, when the workload (multi-tenant conversational assistant, 128,824 keys, Gini 0.756, 80.8% multi-turn) has substantial recoverable per-business reuse. CacheRoute's own framing states the deployment question is a "measurable tradeoff: whether the recoverable hit-rate increase outweighs the residual load skew" — not a verdict that affinity routing is unreliable in general.

### Position B: Affinity concentrates load and can cut capacity below flat balancing

The strongest version of this case rests on CacheRoute's negative-regime data (32B, two independently derived aggregate distributions: capacity falls to 0.50-0.67× Flat-LB when affinity only raises hit rate from 1.1% to 11.8%) and its mechanism ablation (affinity-alone pushes imbalance to 3.46× with *zero* capacity gain over Flat-LB — hit rate goes up, capacity does not, until balanced placement is added). DualMap's independent production-trace evidence ("Cache Affinity alone collapsing under skewed Tool&Agent prefixes") and TokenLake's architectural diagnosis (instance-local routing structurally over-utilizes hit-rate-heavy replicas) both corroborate that this is not an artifact of CacheRoute's specific setup — it recurs across at least three independent research groups (Meta, Huawei/HUST, Peking University/StepFun) studying different systems. llm-d's own maintainers concede the mechanism directly: the "sticky-until-saturated" post names hot-spotting as one of exactly two failure modes that forced a router redesign away from the prior weighted-blend default.

## Where llm-d's actual gate sits relative to CacheRoute's negative regime

This is the load-bearing question the loci-analyst flagged, and the mechanism evidence supports a specific, non-hedged answer: **llm-d's current default is architecturally closer to CacheRoute's winning configuration (affinity + balanced placement + a release valve) than to the naive/pure-affinity configuration CacheRoute shows losing to flat load balancing — but it lacks two structural protections CacheRoute has, and this gap is untested for llm-d specifically.**

Three concrete mechanism-level reasons support the first half of that claim:

1. **llm-d never ships affinity-only routing.** Every matched configuration pairs `prefix-cache-affinity-filter` with a downstream load scorer (`token-load-scorer` or `active-request-scorer`) that picks the least-loaded *candidate within the warm set*. This is structurally the CacheRoute "affinity + placement" row (imbalance 1.24×, capacity at the sweep ceiling), not the "affinity alone" row (imbalance 3.46×, capacity flat vs. Flat-LB) that CacheRoute shows losing. CacheRoute's own paper states the mechanistic reason balanced placement is required: "Locality alone does not help once a queue dominates the tail" — and llm-d's design already assumes this by construction; it was never a one-signal system in production, only briefly a weighted blend without a filter.

2. **llm-d's admission criterion is per-request, not per-aggregate-key, and self-limits under low reuse.** CacheRoute admits a *key* to a stable warm set based on its aggregate rate λ_b, regardless of how much of any individual request is actually cacheable — this is precisely how its 32B negative regime arises: keys get admitted and pinned, but the individual requests under those keys only clear 1.1%-11.8% hit rate, so the pinning buys little while still concentrating load. llm-d's `affinityThreshold=0.8` instead scores *each request's own prefix-match fraction* against each endpoint; if no endpoint clears 0.8, "all endpoints pass through and the downstream scorer operates on the full set" — i.e., the router degrades to pure load balancing automatically. The corpus has direct evidence this happens in practice: on the decode-bound "reasoning" workload, affinity scores never clear 0.8 (a 250-token shared prompt against a ~1000-token user prompt caps affinity at 0.25), so "the filter passes all endpoints through" and the `active-request-scorer` runs on the unrestricted set "as if [the] filter weren't present." This is a structural difference from CacheRoute's aggregate-popularity admission, and it points toward llm-d being less likely to enter CacheRoute's specific 32B failure mode (low per-request recoverable content but still-committed pinning) — though this has not been stress-tested at CacheRoute's scale (128K keys, Gini 0.756) for llm-d specifically.

3. **The saturation override is demonstrated, empirically, to engage under real concentration pressure.** The b2b-saas benchmark is a small-scale analog of exactly the skewed-key regime CacheRoute studies (aggregate cacheable content exceeding aggregate per-endpoint capacity), and the gate is shown firing at QPS 50+ (hit rate drops 90-95%→70-73% as the valve redistributes load away from saturated warm endpoints). This is evidence the release valve is not merely theoretical.

Two gaps temper this into "medium confidence" rather than "resolved":

- **No shadow-replay-equivalent pre-deployment gate.** CacheRoute's deployment checklist requires measuring, before enabling affinity, whether a shadow replay shows capacity or p99 actually improves over Flat-LB — "a cache-hit increase is not enough." llm-d's mechanism is reactive/online only: it always tries affinity first per-request and only escapes via the saturation override once load-based TTFT estimates diverge. There is no analog of CacheRoute's ex-ante regime detection. If a workload sits in CacheRoute's 32B "loses" regime (small but nonzero recoverable reuse, moderate skew, capacity 0.50-0.67× Flat-LB) without ever reaching the token-in-flight saturation threshold, llm-d's design has no mechanism to detect and disable affinity routing before the fact — only the per-request 0.8 threshold and the reactive override, neither of which corresponds to CacheRoute's ex-ante capacity check.
- **No LPT-style formal balance guarantee.** llm-d's token-load-scorer is a greedy per-request pick of the least-loaded warm candidate (closer to power-of-two-choices restricted to a warm set) rather than CacheRoute's offline longest-processing-time-first batch placement, which has provable balance properties over a planning interval. The greedy online approach worked in the benchmarked workloads but has no analog of CacheRoute's demonstrated 1.24× imbalance bound.

Neither gap has been tested against llm-d specifically — the sticky-until-saturated benchmarks (code-generation, reasoning, b2b-saas) do not include a CacheRoute-style large-fleet, low-recoverable-reuse, high-key-count adversarial workload. This is the most direct evidence gap in the corpus for this locus.

## Committed position

The dialectical framing of "does affinity help or hurt" is itself partly a category error: CacheRoute's own paper does not claim affinity routing is unreliable in general, it claims the benefit is conditional on whether recoverable hit-rate gain outweighs residual load skew, and both corpus sides are consistent with that single underlying model rather than contradicting each other — they simply sit at different points on that tradeoff axis (the Red Hat benchmark's multi-turn continuity workload has abundant recoverable reuse; CacheRoute's 32B negative workloads have almost none). Given that reframe, the substantive question for the report is where llm-d's actual shipped mechanism sits on that axis, and the evidence supports a specific, prescriptive answer: llm-d's current default (`prefix-cache-affinity-filter` + `token-load-scorer`/`active-request-scorer`, threshold `affinityThreshold=0.8`, saturation gate `τ_sat = peakPrefillThroughput × maxTTFTPenaltyMs`) is architecturally engineered against exactly the mechanism CacheRoute, DualMap, and TokenLake independently document — it never ships affinity-only routing, its per-request (not per-aggregate-key) admission threshold self-degrades to load-only balancing under low reusable-content workloads, and its saturation override is empirically shown firing under concentration pressure in the b2b-saas benchmark. This puts llm-d's design closer to CacheRoute's winning "affinity + balanced placement" configuration than to the naive pinned-affinity configuration CacheRoute shows losing to Flat-LB by 0.50-0.67×. But llm-d has no pre-deployment shadow-replay-equivalent gate and no formal (LPT-style) balance guarantee, and its benchmarks to date do not include a CacheRoute-scale, low-recoverable-reuse adversarial workload — so "llm-d's mechanism avoids CacheRoute's specific negative regime" is an architecturally well-supported inference, not a demonstrated result.

- **Position:** llm-d's shipped affinity+load-scorer+saturation-gate design is structurally positioned to avoid CacheRoute's capacity-loss regime in most production traffic (because it never runs affinity-only and self-degrades under low per-request reuse), but this has not been empirically validated against CacheRoute's specific adversarial conditions (large fleet, low absolute recoverable reuse, high key-count skew), so the claim "llm-d avoids the CacheRoute failure mode" should be reported as architecturally well-grounded, not benchmark-confirmed.
- **Confidence:** medium. High confidence on the mechanism-comparison claims (these are direct reads of primary source text on both sides); medium confidence on the overall verdict because it rests on an architectural inference bridging two systems that were never benchmarked against each other, and because the b2b-saas workload — while suggestive — has only 150 distinct prompts and 8 endpoints, far short of CacheRoute's 128,824-key, 30-destination adversarial test.
- **Boundary conditions:** This position applies to llm-d's *current* default (`prefix-cache-affinity-filter` + matched scorer, post-0.9 "sticky-until-saturated" design) — it does NOT apply to the prior "legacy blend" default (`prefix-cache-scorer` weight-3 + queue/kv/lru scorers, no filter, no saturation override), which the maintainers themselves replaced partly because of unpredictable hot-spotting behavior, and which is structurally closer to CacheRoute's naive "affinity-only" failure case since prefix locality was enforced only via scorer weight with no explicit release mechanism. It also assumes the operator has correctly calibrated `peakPrefillThroughput`/τ for their (model, accelerator, engine) — the sticky-until-saturated source is explicit that an uncalibrated or transplanted τ from a different hardware path can be off by ~1.9-2.5×, at which point the gate either releases too early (giving up affinity that would have helped) or too late (letting queueing exceed the SLO ceiling).
- **What would change this position:** A published benchmark of llm-d's router against a CacheRoute-style workload (tens of thousands of low-rate keys, Gini coefficient >0.7, absolute recoverable-reuse share in the single digits of percent) showing llm-d's capacity falling below flat load balancing would directly falsify the "architecturally avoids" claim and confirm llm-d inherits CacheRoute's negative regime despite the structural differences. Conversely, such a benchmark showing llm-d holding parity or beating Flat-LB in that regime would upgrade this from "architecturally well-grounded" to "empirically confirmed."
- **Evidence weight:** 2 primary mechanism sources (sticky-until-saturated blog + its griment digest) establish llm-d's actual gate mechanics; 1 rigorous large-scale empirical paper (CacheRoute) establishes the negative regime and its cause; 2 independent corroborating papers (DualMap, TokenLake) confirm the underlying failure mode recurs across systems; 1 vendor benchmark (Red Hat) demonstrates the positive case at small scale. Zero sources directly benchmark llm-d against a CacheRoute-style adversarial workload — this is an inference from mechanism comparison, not a head-to-head result, and the report should say so explicitly rather than presenting the "llm-d is safe" conclusion as demonstrated fact.

## Open questions

- No source in the corpus benchmarks llm-d's router directly against a CacheRoute-style large-fleet, low-recoverable-reuse, high-key-count skewed workload. This is the single biggest gap for confirming (or falsifying) the committed position.
- Resolved during this investigation: the shipped-default question is now confirmed against primary GitHub source (v0.10.0 Godoc + Sep 2026 README), not just the blog post — see "What the new sources say" above.
- CacheRoute's paper explicitly separates "recoverable reuse" from "raw prefix length" — llm-d's affinity score is computed from raw prompt-token overlap, not a separately-measured "recoverable" reuse fraction net of retrieval-block churn (CacheRoute's own workload showed a retrieved few-shot block breaks exact-prefix match in 67% of consecutive request pairs). Whether llm-d's per-request affinity scoring already nets out this kind of churn, or would be fooled by it, is not addressed in any corpus source.
- No source quantifies llm-d's load-imbalance ratio (analogous to CacheRoute's 3.46×/1.24× or DualMap's SLO-violation rate) under sustained skew — TTFT and hit-rate are reported, but not a direct imbalance metric across replicas.
