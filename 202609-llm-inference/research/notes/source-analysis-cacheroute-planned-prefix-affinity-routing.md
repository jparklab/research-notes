# Source Analysis — CacheRoute: Planned Prefix-Affinity Routing for Large-Scale LLM Serving

**Original source:** [[cacheroute-planned-prefix-affinity-routingfor-large-scale-llm-serving]]
**Source type:** paper (arXiv preprint, Meta, Aug 2026)
**Source word count:** 6696
**Your judgment:** The strongest adversarial/negative-result evidence in the corpus on when cache-affinity KV routing fails or actively costs capacity — it does not evaluate llm-d by name, but it evaluates the exact class of mechanism llm-d's endpoint-picker/scheduler implements (prefix-cache-aware, reuse-vs-load routing) and reports the specific failure regimes (stale state, burstiness, low-reuse workloads) that a depth investigator would need to check against llm-d's own design choices.

*Suggested by [[cacheroute-planned-prefix-affinity-routingfor-large-scale-llm-serving]] — source analyst's digest of the full source body*

## Thesis / Central claim
Cache-affinity routing is not unconditionally beneficial: it trades cache locality against load balance, and that trade only pays off when a workload has enough *recoverable* prefix-reuse to outweigh the load skew that pinning introduces. CacheRoute — a periodic, offline-planned router that admits only high-rate keys into a stable warm-prefix assignment, sizes each key's destination count to cap expected load, and places assignments via longest-processing-time-first (LPT) scheduling — beats reactive cache-aware routers (its reimplementations of Preble and a DualMap-style policy) by a wide margin on a favorable workload, but the paper's second half is devoted to demonstrating two real workloads (32B model, two distinct aggregate traffic distributions) where the same mechanism *loses* to plain load balancing. The paper's explicit prescription is: never enable affinity routing from workload statistics alone — gate every deployment behind a shadow replay, because model size does not predict which regime a workload falls into.

## Methodology / Basis of claims
- **Hardware benchmarking on real GPU fleets**, not simulation, for all headline numbers: Llama-3.3-70B in fp8 on 30 tensor-parallel-2 destinations (60 H100 GPUs, each destination exposing 40,071 measured KV blocks ≈ 641K tokens); a mechanism-isolation testbed on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct bf16 on 30 single-H100 destinations; negative-regime testbed on Qwen3-32B bf16 on 30 MP2 destinations (60 H100).
- **Workload construction**: "semi-synthetic aggregate" traffic replayed from de-identified operational telemetry (per-key rates, inter-arrival gaps, popularity distribution) of a multi-tenant conversational-assistant/customer-support service — no raw request content. Primary distribution: 128,824 opaque business keys, Gini 0.756, top 4% of keys = 47% of requests, median per-key inter-arrival CV = 1.93, 80.8% multi-turn. Stable reusable segment is only ~180 tokens (15%) of a ~1.2K-token prompt; a retrieved few-shot block breaks exact-prefix match in 67% of consecutive request pairs for the same key.
- **Statistical design**: paired seeds per policy (5 seeds flagship, 8 seeds top-256 confirmation, 3 seeds for secondary studies), 95% Student-t CIs, SLO capacity defined as highest offered-QPS ladder point with p99 ≤ threshold and failure rate ≤5%; explicit left-/right-censoring discipline when a ladder doesn't resolve a knee.
- **Baselines**: Flat-LB (power-of-two-choices), sticky consistent hashing, consistent hashing with bounded loads (CHWBL, ε=0.25), a DualMap-style two-candidate cache/load policy, and a Preble-style prefix-history + live-load policy (1.5 load-cap factor). **Load-bearing caveat the paper itself states**: Preble, DualMap, and CHWBL are the authors' own common-harness *reimplementations*, not the original codebases — "our absolute results should not be read as reproductions of their original systems."
- **Ablation methodology**: an 8B synthetic-whale workload isolates affinity-alone vs. affinity+LPT-placement vs. affinity+replication, allowing the paper to attribute capacity gains to specific mechanisms rather than the bundle.

## Key findings / Claims (with specific numbers where present)
1. **Headline positive result (70B fp8, 60 H100, top-128 active set, primary distribution, 5 seeds)**: CacheRoute sustains 176±11 QPS at p99≤3.5s vs. Preble's 76±11 QPS (2.3x) and Flat-LB's 42±20 QPS (4.2x). CacheRoute's served KV-cache hit rate is 93.2±0.5% vs. Flat-LB's 64.1±1.3%, Preble's 72.0±0.7%, CHWBL's 75.6±0.8%, Sticky's 87.3±2.4%, DualMap's 88.7±1.9%.
2. **Hit rate alone does not predict capacity.** Sticky (87.3% hit) and DualMap (88.7% hit) both have *worse* p99 at 100 QPS (8.5s and 5.3s respectively) than Preble/CHWBL, which have lower hit rates (72.0%, 75.6%) but more even queues. "Locality alone does not help once a queue dominates the tail." CacheRoute's advantage is explicitly attributed to combining high reuse *with* balanced placement, not reuse alone.
3. **Active-set breadth changes the ranking.** At top-256 (wider active set) the advantage shrinks to 1.33x at 3.5s vs. the 2.3x at top-128; on a second, independently collected traffic distribution, all three balanced cache-aware policies *tie* at top-128 (100 QPS) and separate only once the active set (top-256) outgrows the warm-slot allocation, where CacheRoute reaches 160 QPS vs. 100 QPS best baseline (1.6x). This means the benchmark advantage is workload/config-dependent, not a fixed multiplier.
4. **Mechanism decomposition (8B synthetic-whale ablation, top-128, 3 seeds)**: affinity alone raises KV hit from 56% to 88% but pushes load imbalance to 3.46x, and capacity stays flat at 240 QPS (no benefit despite higher hit rate). Adding load-proportional replication reduces imbalance to 2.60x but capacity is still unchanged. Only adding LPT placement drops imbalance to 1.24x and moves capacity to the sweep ceiling (≥500 QPS, right-censored). **This is the mechanistic core of the paper's argument: affinity/locality and load balance are separable and must both be engineered — locality gains are worthless, or actively harmful, without a load-aware placement algorithm on top.**
5. **Explicit negative/counterexample regimes (32B model, two independent semi-synthetic aggregate workloads, matched runs)**: on "Aggregate A–32B," affinity raises KV hit only from 1.1% to 11.8%, and capacity falls to 0.50–0.67x of flat load balancing — a straight capacity *loss* from enabling cache-affinity routing. On "Aggregate B–32B," affinity raises hit from 0.8% to 8.5% and merely ties flat LB at the 5s SLO. Quote: "when affinity recovers too little KV work, its residual load skew reduces or erases the improvement." **Model size alone does not predict which regime a workload falls into** — the 32B counterexamples and the 70B/8B positive cases are not distinguished by parameter count but by how much *recoverable* prefix-reuse the traffic actually contains.
6. **Burstiness degrades capacity by one ladder step.** On 70B fp8/top-128, a Gamma arrival process matched to the workload's marginal CV=1.9 cuts CacheRoute's 3.5s capacity from 180 to 160 QPS (KV-hit falls 94%→90%); Flat-LB stays pinned at 30 QPS regardless (i.e., CacheRoute's advantage over Flat-LB is preserved under burstiness even though its absolute capacity drops). A separate moving-block-bootstrap of 22,639 real inter-arrival gaps (empirical CV=2.73) leaves CacheRoute at 180 QPS/93% hit while Flat-LB becomes left-censored below 30 QPS.
7. **Stale routing plans measurably degrade performance, and refreshing them is also costly.** Perturbing the rate vector with a lognormal random walk (σ=0.3/step) over 6 intervals and comparing a fresh vs. one-interval-stale plan at 160 QPS: recomputing LPT from scratch changes 94.5% of key-to-destination *sets* even though only 1.1% of keys change their assignment *count* (i.e., most churn is destination reshuffling among equally-loaded servers, not real demand shifts). The stale plan loses on average 1.0 percentage point of KV hit and 192ms of p99; the worst single transition loses 3.0 points of KV hit and 858ms of p99. Installing the freshly recomputed plan, in turn, causes a transient **13.6-point KV-hit drop** during cache rewarming. **Explicit prescription**: "A deployment should therefore preserve placements until the measured staleness penalty exceeds the warm-up penalty" — the paper states they have not implemented a churn-aware replanner, i.e., this is an open problem, not a solved one, even in the paper's own reference design.
8. **Analytic residency prediction fails — this is a direct rebuttal to any router design that tries to predict cache hit/eviction from block counts or occupancy models.** A single-characteristic-time occupancy model, tested against the real 70B fp8 engine with 40,071 measured KV blocks/destination, missed served hit rate by 14.3 percentage points at the median and 44.7 points at p90; the real residency curve "drops sharply and then plateaus, a shape outside the tested model family." The paper explicitly declines to attribute this shape to any engine mechanism (batching, block quantization, etc.) without direct evidence, and uses this failure as the justification for requiring **shadow replay** (running the candidate routing plan without serving its responses to users, then comparing served KV hit/load/p99 against baseline) as the deployment gate rather than any predictive/analytic sizing method.
9. **fp16 vs fp8 cache-pressure comparison**: under fp16 (roughly 2x memory pressure vs fp8), no policy meets the fp8 study's 3.5s SLO at all — Flat-LB's KV hit collapses to 19.5±2.1% (vs 64.1% at fp8) while Sticky and CacheRoute hold at 77.3% and 76.4% respectively. This is reported only as supporting cache-pressure evidence, explicitly not combined with the fp8 headline multiplier.
10. **An earlier apparent anomaly (high failure rates at top-256) did not reproduce** in a dedicated 8-seed confirmation run — the paper flags this transparently rather than suppressing it, and does not treat it as a repeatable policy reversal.

## Load-bearing citations / sources this source depends on
- **Kwon et al. 2023 (PagedAttention/vLLM, SOSP)** and **Zheng et al. 2024 (SGLang, NeurIPS)** — establishes that prefix-KV reuse within one engine is now standard; CacheRoute's contribution is explicitly framed as "which destination receives the request," not engine-level caching.
- **Srivatsa et al. 2024 (Preble, arXiv:2407.00023)** — the strongest baseline in this paper's own evaluation; a reactive prefix-history + live-load router. **Directly relevant to llm-d**: this is the closest published system to llm-d's prefix-cache-aware scorer design, and CacheRoute claims a 2.3x capacity advantage over its own reimplementation of Preble on the favorable workload.
- **Yuan et al. 2026 (DualMap, arXiv:2602.06502)** — "combines static and dynamic mappings," the second reactive/hybrid baseline; ties CacheRoute at narrow active sets and loses at wide active sets.
- **Mirrokni et al. 2018 (consistent hashing with bounded loads, SODA)** and **Karger et al. 1997 (consistent hashing, STOC)** — the classical stable-affinity-with-bounded-load lineage that CacheRoute's LPT-placement idea descends from.
- **Graham 1969 (LPT scheduling bound, SIAM J. Appl. Math)** and **Mitzenmacher et al. 2001 (power-of-two-choices survey)** — the two textbook scheduling primitives CacheRoute composes; paper is explicit that neither is novel and Graham's approximation bound does not actually apply to CacheRoute's split-job/distinct-destination variant.
- **Qin et al. 2025 (Mooncake, ACM TOS)** and **Hu et al. 2024 (MemServe, arXiv:2406.17565)** — named as a *different* architectural approach (moving/pooling KV state across servers rather than ingress-affinity) that the paper says "can still use an ingress plan to avoid prefill that routing would otherwise repeat" — i.e., framed as complementary, not competing.
- **Sun et al. 2024 (Llumnix, OSDI)**, **Zhong et al. 2024 (DistServe, OSDI)**, **Patel et al. 2024 (Splitwise, ISCA)** — named in Related Work as adjacent placement/migration/disaggregation systems, not benchmarked against.
- **Flag for the orchestrator**: the underlying traffic telemetry (128,824 business keys, Gini 0.756, etc.) is proprietary Meta operational data, released only as replay statistics — not a reproducible public dataset. All headline numbers are non-replicable outside Meta's infrastructure; independent verification is impossible from the paper alone.

## Caveats, limitations, contradictions
- **The paper never mentions llm-d, the Gateway API Inference Extension, or the endpoint-picker architecture by name.** It is a general academic contribution to the prefix-affinity-routing literature, not an evaluation of any specific open-source project. Any inference to llm-d's specific design must be drawn by analogy (llm-d's scheduler/EPP uses cache-aware, reuse-vs-load scoring conceptually similar to the paper's "Preble-style" and "DualMap-style" baselines), not by direct citation.
- **Self-disclosed reimplementation caveat**: Preble, DualMap, and CHWBL results in this paper are the authors' own common-harness reimplementations, explicitly *not* validated reproductions of the original published systems. Any claim that "CacheRoute beats Preble 2.3x" should be read as "beats our reimplementation of Preble under our harness," not as a contested claim against the original Preble paper's own reported numbers.
- **Right-censoring throughout the 8B study**: several 8B knees hit the 500-QPS sweep ceiling and are right-censored, meaning CacheRoute's true advantage at 8B scale may be understated but is not resolved by the experiment.
- **Wide-active-set and second-distribution results are notably weaker than the flagship 2.3x** (1.33x, 1.6x, or outright ties) — the headline number is the best case, not the typical case, and the paper is transparent about this in its own "claim-to-evidence map" appendix.
- **fp16 comparison provides no capacity multiplier** (all policies miss the SLO) — it only supports a qualitative cache-pressure argument.
- **The paper explicitly refuses to generalize past two workload distributions**: "two distributions cannot represent every market or application," and equal-slot admission "assumes roughly uniform stable-prefix sizes" — it does not handle heterogeneous long-context reusable-prefix sizes (no byte-aware admission).
- **Self-contradiction risk flagged by the paper itself**: an earlier five-seed top-256 sweep showed anomalous failures that did not reproduce in an eight-seed confirmation; the paper retains but does not rely on the anomaly — a good-faith transparency move but also evidence that even this careful benchmark has run-to-run noise large enough to produce spurious "failures."
- **The analytic residency-prediction failure (Appendix H) is itself a limitation the paper turns into a design principle**: it could not build a predictive model of KV cache occupancy/hit-rate from block counts even with instrumentation passing 7 isolation checks — meaning no static config-based heuristic (which is what many production routers, including simpler versions of cache-affinity routing, might rely on) can be trusted without empirical shadow-replay validation.
- **Replanning is an acknowledged open gap**: "We have not implemented a churn-aware replanner" — the paper's own reference design does not solve the stale-vs-fresh-plan tradeoff it documents; it only measures the tradeoff and recommends a heuristic (don't refresh until staleness cost exceeds rewarming cost).

## Relevance to research_query
This source is highly relevant as **adversarial/boundary-condition evidence** for the research_query "how llm-d manages KV cache and routes based on the cache," even though it never names llm-d. llm-d's endpoint-picker/scheduler implements prefix-cache-aware, reuse-vs-load scoring — architecturally the same category of mechanism as this paper's "Preble-style" and "DualMap-style" baselines (both of which the paper implements and beats on a favorable workload, though narrowly or not at all on other workloads). This source supplies the counter-evidence a fair account of llm-d's routing design needs: (1) precise, quantified conditions under which cache-affinity routing *reduces* capacity relative to flat load balancing (32B workloads where affinity only lifts hit rate from ~1% to ~9-12%, yielding 0.50-1.0x capacity vs. flat LB) — directly bears on whether llm-d's KV-aware routing could backfire on low-reuse workloads; (2) a quantified accounting of stale-routing-plan cost (up to 3.0 points KV-hit and 858ms p99 loss) versus fresh-plan cache-rewarming cost (13.6-point transient KV-hit drop) — directly informs how llm-d's *event-driven* (near-real-time KV-block-event) routing state design should be judged against this paper's periodic/offline-planned alternative: llm-d's approach trades planning overhead for freshness, and this paper quantifies what staleness would cost if llm-d's event stream lagged; (3) quantified burstiness sensitivity (Gamma CV=1.9 process cutting capacity by one ladder step, 180→160 QPS) that is a reasonable proxy for what to expect if llm-d's routing state updates cannot keep pace with bursty traffic; (4) the mechanistic finding that hit-rate improvement alone does not predict capacity (Sticky/DualMap have higher hit rates than Preble/CacheRoute-baseline but worse p99) is a load-bearing corrective for any llm-d documentation or benchmark that reports only KV-cache hit rate without pairing it against load/tail-latency — a critic reviewing an llm-d draft should check whether hit-rate-only claims are being treated as sufficient evidence of routing quality. The paper's central operational prescription — "gate cache-affinity deployment with shadow replay, not workload statistics or model size" — is a strong, source-independent recommendation any assessment of llm-d's default routing configuration should weigh.

## Extracted quotes
> "Cache-blind balancing disperses that reuse; fixed affinity preserves it but can overload a server. CacheRoute resolves this tradeoff with a periodic routing plan."
Frames the load-bearing tension (locality vs. load balance) that motivates the entire paper and that any KV-cache-aware router, including llm-d's, must resolve.

> "Cache-aware routers instead react to current cache and load state [Srivatsa et al.(2024), Yuan et al.(2026)]; once a destination fills, however, spilling the next request also forfeits the warm prefix."
This is the paper's explicit critique of the reactive-routing category that llm-d's scheduler design most closely resembles — a direct challenge worth checking against llm-d's own spillover/fallback behavior.

> "A cache-hit increase is not enough; the plan is enabled only when p99 or capacity improves."
The paper's core deployment-gating principle — a hit-rate metric alone is insufficient evidence of routing benefit.

> "Aggregate A–32B, affinity moves KV hit only from 1.1% to 11.8%. That improvement does not outweigh the remaining skew, and capacity falls to 0.50–0.67× Flat-LB."
The sharpest quantified counterexample: cache-affinity routing actively halving capacity relative to flat load balancing on a real (if de-identified) 32B workload.

> "A deployment should therefore preserve placements until the measured staleness penalty exceeds the warm-up penalty. We have not implemented a churn-aware replanner."
Directly bears on how llm-d should be assessed on routing-state freshness vs. stability tradeoffs — the paper flags this as an open problem even in its own system.

> "Physical block capacity and aggregate rates are therefore insufficient for pre-deployment sizing on this engine. The deployment interface includes a shadow replay that measures served KV hit and p99 for the candidate assignment."
Rebuts any static/analytic approach to predicting cache behavior from configuration alone — relevant to evaluating whether llm-d's routing heuristics (or any KV-cache scorer) can be trusted without live validation.

> "Model size by itself does not distinguish these outcomes."
Explicitly warns against using model size as a proxy for whether cache-affinity routing will help — relevant caveat for any generalization from llm-d's own benchmark model sizes to other deployments.
