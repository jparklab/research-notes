# Interim report: end-to-end-reference-architecture

**Locus question:** How do the pieces actually wire together in a working internal platform — what sits between gateway and engine, how weights get distributed and loaded, how a request flows end to end?
**Flavor:** synthesis

## What the corpus already said

The width sweep had already isolated every individual component this locus needs to assemble, as discrete consensus claims rather than a wired system: vLLM as the near-universal engine (`vllm-is-the-default-engine`, 11 independent voices), Kubernetes as the assumed substrate (`kubernetes-is-the-substrate`), the gateway/vendor-abstraction layer as the one place every operator writes bespoke code (`gateway-is-where-internal-teams-build`, 5 voices: Uber, Atlassian, Thomson Reuters, Shopify, LinkedIn), KV-cache reuse as "the reason the orchestration layer exists at all" (`kv-cache-reuse-is-the-dominant-lever`, 6 voices), and FP8-via-llm-compressor as the routine quantization path (`quantization-is-routine`). The contradiction graph's `oss-control-plane-sufficiency` cluster had already located the fault line precisely: "the engine and the control plane have commoditized into OSS, while the gateway/policy/metering/eval surface has not." What none of this assembled was the actual wiring — what literally sits between a client call and a token, and what the weight gets from a registry to GPU VRAM. That is the gap this locus closes, using the named deployments the orchestrator specified plus two additional vault notes the search surfaced that resolve the cold-start chain directly ([[llm-autoscaling-and-cold-starts-in-kubernetes-architecture-keda-metrics-model-we]], which supplies the actual "five sequential phases" the brief referenced, previously only gestured at) and confirms the practitioner reference stack in full first-person detail ([[self-hosted-llm-stack-a-solid-foundation-for-an-open-weight-platform-built-to-ev]]).

No new fetches were needed for this locus — every component the brief named was already a full-body vault note (several under topic-only tags not captured by the corpus_tag filter, confirming the orchestrator's caveat). This is an assembly job and the assembly is now done entirely from primary, first-person text.

## What the sources say

**Thomson Reuters** ([[thomson-continual-learning-of-frontier-models-for]], digested in [[source-analysis-thomson-continual-learning-of-frontier-models-for-sovereignai]]): "Our LLM orchestration and inference platform is built on Kubernetes, with a routing, authorisation, and rate-limiting plane implemented in Rust, using vLLM as an inference backend. It operates over 11 independent clusters spread across 4 geographies and 2 cloud providers" — with routing clusters and inference clusters kept physically separate for blast-radius containment. Production scale: "more than 40M requests and processed 370B tokens" since April 2026, 99.9% success rate, 19.7% cost saving vs. third-party inference for GLM-5.2. The engine customization is scoped and stated: "We customise vLLM to support novel models and features prior to availability of longer-term support in the vLLM upstream," specifically custom logits processors for client-side thinking budgets and custom reasoning parsers for models without native reasoning traces. The cold-start fix, quoted exactly: moving model weights to an in-memory volume plus "a faster-loading library (e.g., instanttensor)" cut cold-start "often 20 minutes or longer" down to "approximately five minutes, and often less" — done explicitly to avoid cascading-failure risk from slow replica starts. Quantization is FP8 via vLLM's own `llm-compressor`, with `lm_head` and MoE routing/gating layers deliberately left unquantized.

**T-Tech** (arXiv 2609.01572, [[from-production-traffic-to-post-training-building-a-self-hosted-llm]]): the paper's own production numbers, quoted directly — "the merged checkpoint serves 116M requests per month, 45 average and 110 peak requests per second, from over 200 internal services on single-GPU FP8 replicas behind vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023), 16 to 48 pods, with 95th-percentile latency of 3.2 s and time-to-first-token of 0.3 s." Cost outcome: "reduces per-token cost by 2.8 to 3.9× on input and output, and up to 4 to 9× for services that previously ran the largest platform models." The paper is explicit that its own novelty is entirely upstream of serving — traffic-stratified benchmarking, per-axis RL experts (instruction-following, function-calling, alignment), weight-space SLERP merging — and the serving engine itself is unmodified vLLM.

**Tesla** (via [[production-grade-llm-inference-at-scale-with-kserve-llm-d-and-vllm-llm-d]], the llm-d project's own account of the Tesla deployment, acknowledged by name): the naive-deployment failure sequence is given in the operator's own words, not inferred: "We initially approached this challenge with a straightforward vLLM deployment wrapped in a Kubernetes StatefulSet," which hit three named walls — **storage drag** ("Relying on sluggish network storage (NFS) for these massive safetensors was a non-starter"), **infrastructure lock-in** ("Switching to local LVM persistent volumes solved the speed problem but created a rigid node-to-pod affinity. A single hardware failure meant a manual intervention to delete the Persistent Volume Claim (PVC) and reschedule the pod"), and **cache-blind load balancing** ("a simple round-robin load-balancing strategy is fundamentally inefficient for LLMs... it fails to utilize the critical KV-cache on the GPU"). The fix was KServe + llm-d + vLLM with Envoy / Envoy AI Gateway / Gateway API Inference Extension providing prefix-cache-aware routing, measured on one deployment at "a 3x improvement in output tokens/s and a 2x reduction in time to first token (TTFT)" (Llama 3.1 70B, 4 MI300X, `tensor-parallel-size=4`). Tesla's production use also directly motivated a KServe upstream change — `storageInitializer made optional` (kserve#4970) — "enabling RunAI Model Streamer as an alternative to the default storage initializer," tying this deployment straight into the weight-loading chain below.

**Netflix** ([[in-house-llm-serving-at-netflix-by-ai-platforms-model-runtime-team-and-by-netfli]]): the most granular first-person wiring account in the corpus. Architecture, in the post's own words: "Larger models need GPUs — the serving system handles pre- and post-processing locally but delegates inference to a remote service, **Model Scoring Service (MSS)**... with NVIDIA Triton Inference Server underneath managing model loading, batching, and GPU scheduling. On top of Triton sits a Java control plane that handles deployment, versioning, health checking, autoscaling, and multi-region rollout." The engine choice is a documented migration, not an original decision: "The platform was originally built on TensorRT-LLM... By summer 2025... we re-benchmarked against this mix and selected vLLM as our paved-path engine on operational fit" (custom-architecture loading without recompilation, logits-processor extensibility, debuggability, practitioner familiarity). Packaging is Triton's vLLM backend (dynamic I/O specs from a JSON config) rather than the Python backend, chosen explicitly so "Models and frontend evolve independently" — but this created a real production failure mode: "Triton's vLLM backend is compiled against a specific vLLM API surface. When the two drift... the backend fails to load entirely," forcing the platform to pin versions. The API surface is OpenAI-compatible-plus-gRPC by design, reusing NVIDIA's Triton OpenAI-compatible frontend, patched in-house (git-subtree) because `response_format` was silently dropped before reaching vLLM's guided decoding. Cold-start handling: "We materialize models on Amazon FSx at the time of model announcement, so warm starts hit a high-performance file system instead of object storage" — a pre-pull strategy distinct from either streaming or snapshotting. Deployment/rollout is a deliberate two-strategy choice: **Red-Black** (new version scales up as old scales down, atomic rollback on failure — the default) versus **Versioned** (every (modelId, modelVersion) pair gets an independent deployment, used only "for the rare cases where a breaking interface change is unavoidable," at a stated GPU-cost premium during overlap). Observability required a custom fix identical in shape to the AIBrix/contradiction-graph finding: "Triton's built-in bridge surfaces only 9 of 40+ vLLM metrics," so Netflix built an HTTP proxy merging both Prometheus sources.

**Atlassian** (two companion posts: [[atlassians-inference-engine-our-self-hosted-ai-inference-service-inside-atlassia]] and [[atlassians-ai-gateway-best-in-class-model-garden-inside-atlassian]]): a clean two-plane split that corroborates the gateway/engine boundary seen everywhere else. The Inference Engine ("Triton Inference Server, and for GenAI models, TensorRT-LLM and vLLM as backends") is the self-hosted-model serving plane; the separate AI Gateway ("a curated model garden of 20+ LLMs... from 5+ providers") is the vendor-routing-and-fallback plane, explicitly distinct. Orchestration is GitOps-first: "Helm + ArgoCD + Kustomize," Karpenter for node provisioning ("compared to alternatives like Cluster Autoscaler... Karpenter offers faster provisioning"), Istio for ingress ("better control over retries, observability... splitting traffic and shadow deployments"), KEDA for autoscaling on queue duration/latency/GPU utilization, Prometheus+SignalFx for observability. Reported outcomes from the migration off third-party hosting: up to 40% P90 latency reduction and >60% cost reduction for LLM workloads specifically.

**Practitioner reference stack** ([[self-hosted-llm-stack-a-solid-foundation-for-an-open-weight-platform-built-to-ev]], Ogenki/Smana — a named individual practitioner's GitOps repo, not an enterprise deployment, but structurally identical to the enterprise accounts above and useful because it shows every seam explicitly): a `Crossplane` `InferenceService` CRD is the single declared interface — "about a dozen Kubernetes resources are created" from one YAML claim, including a vLLM `Deployment`, an idempotent weight-preloading `Job` ("downloads the ~15 GB of HuggingFace weights to the S3 Files PVC... vLLM pods then mount the volume in seconds"), a KEDA `ScaledObject`, and an `AIGatewayRoute`. The KEDA triggers scale on two vLLM-native signals, not CPU/GPU core usage: `vllm:num_requests_running / maxNumSeqs` at a 70% threshold and `vllm:gpu_cache_usage_perc` at 60% — chosen specifically "to anticipate" queue formation rather than react to it. Karpenter provisions nodes underneath KEDA on a ~60s cycle. The access layer is Envoy AI Gateway (per-model routing on the OpenAI `model` field, bearer-token auth, per-token cost extraction) fronting **vLLM Semantic Router ("Iris")** as a sidecar: a ~100M-parameter mmBERT-derived classifier, served on CPU, that does intent/jailbreak/PII classification and routes a virtual "Mixture-of-Models" endpoint to the right real model — at a measured, stated cost of "~250-300ms of classification," which is why latency-critical paths (IDE autocomplete, sub-200ms p95) use explicit model-name routing instead and bypass the semantic router entirely. Evaluation is Promptfoo as a nightly CronJob whose results are pushed as Prometheus metrics into the same Grafana used for infra metrics.

**Kubernetes-native routing-signal mechanism** ([[beyond-gateway-inference-traffic-control-practice-with]], an independent third-party technical explainer of the Gateway API Inference Extension — not a deployment account but documents the mechanism three of the above deployments actually use): the Endpoint Picker (EPP) sits behind Envoy's `ext-proc` hook and makes per-request routing decisions on real backend telemetry — "KV Cache hit rate, queue length, and GPU utilization" — bound to an `InferencePool` (the backend group) and optionally an `InferenceObjective` (per-request priority: Critical / High / BestEffort, used to shed low-priority load under contention). The source is explicit about maturity: "The current project version is v1.1.x, overall in the Alpha stage. Official recommendation is not to use it directly in production yet" — directly corroborating the contradiction graph's `gateway-governance-neutrality` finding that this layer is standardizing in spec but not yet settled in practice.

**Cold-start / weight-loading chain, resolved from primary sources only:**

- [[github-coreweavetensorizer-module-model-and-tensor-serializationdeserialization]] (CoreWeave's own repo README, primary): "GPT-J (20GB) loads at wire-speed (~5GB/s) on a 40GbE network" and states its own motivation directly — "CoreWeave and our customers use KNative to deploy models as serverless functions. How long a model takes to load is a major factor in the latency of KNative scale-up." Tensorizer does zero-copy, tensor-by-tensor streaming direct from HTTP/S3/disk into GPU memory, decoupling weight updates from container-image rebuilds.
- [[reducing-cold-start-latency-for-llm-inference-with-nvidia-runai-model-streamer-n]] (NVIDIA's own benchmark post, primary): loading a 15GB Llama-3-8B model took **14.34s on GP3 SSD at concurrency 16, 7.53s on IO2 SSD at concurrency 8, and 4.88s on S3 at concurrency 32**, versus 47-48s for the default HF Safetensors loader at concurrency 1 (which has no S3 path at all). The post's own measured throughput ceiling is storage-bound — "~1 GiB/s, the max for GP3 SSD" and "a ceiling at around 2 GiB/s with Model Streamer and 1.6 GiB/s with Tensorizer" on IO2 SSD. **I searched this primary source directly for a "~80 Gbps" figure and it does not appear anywhere in the post** — the only throughput units used throughout are GiB/s and MiB/s, an order of magnitude below Gbps, and the figures are all in single digits to low double digits of GiB/s. This confirms the width sweep's fabrication finding: the ~80 Gbps figure circulating in secondary literature has no basis in NVIDIA's own post.
- [[vllm-sessions-at-pytorch-conference-north-america-2026-pytorch]] (PyTorch Conference session listing, primary program text): fastsafetensors (Takeshi Yoshimura, IBM) — "removes per-tensor copies, coalesces fragmented I/O, and skips host staging. The speakers report 4.8x to 7.5x faster model loading and up to 28 GB/s of NVMe read throughput," with vLLM integration, 3FS integration, and ROCm support named as shipped contributions.
- [[gpu-memory-snapshots-supercharging-sub-second-startup]] (Modal's own blog, primary): I read this directly rather than trusting the secondary citation. Modal's own reported vLLM figure is **"vLLM running Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct would previously take 45s (P0) to startup and now takes 5s (P0)"** using CUDA checkpoint/restore APIs (`cuCheckpointProcessCheckpoint`/`cuCheckpointProcessRestore`) to snapshot GPU+CPU memory state *after* weight load, `torch.compile`, and CUDA graph capture have all completed, then restore that full state on later cold boots. **The "460s→70s" figure attributed to this post in secondary literature does not appear in the primary source at all** — this is a fabrication, confirmed directly against Modal's own text, exactly as the width sweep flagged. The real number is 45s→5s, a 9x reduction, for a much smaller model (0.5B, not a 70B-class model).
- [[autoscaling-with-keda-production-stack]] (vLLM Production Stack's own KEDA tutorial, primary): scale-to-zero requires two triggers bolted together, not one — a queue-depth trigger (`vllm:num_requests_waiting`) plus a second "traffic-based keepalive" trigger (`sum(rate(vllm:num_incoming_requests_total[1m]) > bool 0)`) whose explicit, stated purpose is to "prevent scale-to-zero when traffic exists." This confirms the brief's framing precisely: KEDA's native primitive does not know to stay up during a momentary queue lull, so the project's own tutorial documents the workaround as required, not optional.
- [[llm-autoscaling-and-cold-starts-in-kubernetes-architecture-keda-metrics-model-we]] (llms.blog, a secondary gray-literature source, used here only for structure/vocabulary, not for unverified figures): this is the source of the "five sequential phases" the brief referenced. Its own breakdown: (1) Node Provisioning and Driver Initialization, (2) Container Image Distribution, (3) Weight Transfer to Host Storage, (4) Host RAM→GPU VRAM Loading, (5) Runtime Engine Warmup and CUDA Graph Capture. Phase 1 is accelerator/capacity-adjacent and out of scope per the user's hardware exclusion; phases 2-5 are the pure software path. This source's own per-phase timing ranges and its storage-tier comparison table (EFS 100-250 MB/s → 9.3-23.3 min for a 140GB model; multi-threaded S3 800-1500 MB/s → 1.5-2.9 min; local NVMe 3500-6000 MB/s → 23-40s; host pinned memory 25,000+ MB/s → 5-6s) are **not independently verified against a primary source** and should be read as one practitioner-blog's illustrative modeling, not a measured benchmark — flagged here rather than asserted as fact, in the same spirit as the width sweep's fabrication catches above. What is corroborated independently is the *shape*: Thomson Reuters' own 20min→~5min fix is a pinned-memory-plus-faster-loader move, structurally identical to this source's phase-4 framing, even though the two sources don't share numbers.

## Evidence synthesis

Two architectures, not one, actually recur — and they map cleanly onto the two serving substrates in the corpus. **Substrate A: Kubernetes-native vLLM**, used directly or through a thin control-plane layer (Thomson Reuters, T-Tech, Tesla, the practitioner stack, and most of the contradiction graph's eleven vLLM adopters). **Substrate B: Triton as the shared multi-framework serving backend**, with vLLM or TensorRT-LLM plugged in underneath it as one of several supported backends (Netflix, Atlassian). These are not competing descriptions of the same thing — they are a real fork in the stack driven by whether the organization already had a pre-LLM model-serving platform to fold LLMs into. Netflix's post is explicit about this: MSS already served XGBoost/TensorFlow/PyTorch before LLMs arrived, so the "LLM models should NOT be special snowflakes" design goal forced LLM serving through the same Triton-fronted gRPC path the rest of ML scoring already used. Atlassian's Inference Engine description similarly treats "search models, content moderators" and LLMs as the same platform. Thomson Reuters and T-Tech, by contrast, had no pre-existing non-LLM serving platform to integrate with, so vLLM-direct-on-Kubernetes was the lower-friction choice. This is a genuine architectural fork, and it is explained by organizational history, not by technical merit of one engine over the other — both Netflix and Atlassian explicitly still use vLLM, just one layer further down.

Above the engine, every account independently converges on the same three-plane separation: a **gateway/control plane** that is always bespoke or heavily customized (Rust at Thomson Reuters, a Java control plane at Netflix, a separate AI Gateway at Atlassian, Envoy AI Gateway + Iris at the practitioner stack), an **orchestration layer** that is near-universally Kubernetes plus a node provisioner (Karpenter appears at Atlassian and in the practitioner stack; it is also what llm-d/Tesla and the general cold-start literature assume) plus a pod autoscaler driven by engine-native metrics rather than CPU/GPU core usage (KEDA on `vllm:num_requests_waiting` / `gpu_cache_usage_perc`, independently specified at Atlassian and in the practitioner stack with near-identical metric names), and an **engine layer** that is vLLM everywhere except where a pre-existing multi-framework platform (Triton) already existed. The routing *decision* — which specific pod a request lands on — is made on backend telemetry (KV-cache hit/prefix affinity, queue depth, GPU utilization) rather than on connection count or round-robin, and Tesla's llm-d account gives the only concrete before/after number in the corpus for this specific lever: 3x tokens/s, 2x TTFT improvement from adding prefix-cache-aware routing to a previously round-robin setup.

The weight-distribution/cold-start path resolves into exactly two non-exclusive families, and the corpus is unusually clean about naming the actual engineering tradeoff between them. **Streaming loaders** (Tensorizer, Run:ai Model Streamer, fastsafetensors) attack a single stage — getting bytes from storage into GPU memory faster, skipping the disk round-trip and parallelizing reads — and their own benchmarks are all storage-bandwidth-bound: NVIDIA's own numbers top out around 1-2 GiB/s on commodity cloud block/object storage, regardless of which streaming tool is used, because the bottleneck is the storage tier, not the loader. **Snapshot/restore** (Modal's CUDA checkpoint-based GPU memory snapshots) attacks the entire post-load initialization path at once — weight load, `torch.compile`, CUDA graph capture — by checkpointing all of it after the fact, which is why Modal's own real numbers (45s→5s for a 0.5B model) look more dramatic than any streaming-loader number for a comparable model: streaming only ever removes one stage, snapshotting removes four. Thomson Reuters' production fix sits in neither camp cleanly — pinning weights to an in-memory volume plus a faster loader is closer to the streaming family's goal (faster bytes-to-GPU) but achieved through host-side memory placement rather than a dedicated streaming protocol, and it was sized for the problem that actually mattered to them (production uptime risk from slow replica starts cascading into failures), not for scale-to-zero. This is the key point the fabricated figures obscured: the real primary numbers are heterogeneous by design — different model sizes, different storage tiers, different problems being solved (steady-state replica-restart risk at Thomson Reuters vs. true scale-to-zero at Modal) — and flattening them into one misattributed number erases exactly the distinction that matters for choosing a technique.

## Request-flow account

An internal caller hits a single OpenAI-compatible (or gRPC) endpoint behind the gateway/control plane. The gateway authenticates and rate-limits the caller, applies quota/chargeback policy, and decides vendor-vs-self-hosted routing if that decision is still live at request time (Uber/Atlassian pattern) — distinct from, and upstream of, the within-self-hosted-fleet pod selection. If a semantic/content router sits in the path (Iris pattern), it classifies the prompt (intent, safety, PII) against a small CPU-resident model and resolves a virtual model name to a concrete one; callers that already know which model they want bypass this step by naming the model explicitly, which is the common case for latency-sensitive traffic (IDE autocomplete, agentic tool loops). The resolved request then reaches an endpoint-selection layer — either the bespoke control plane's own logic (Thomson Reuters' Rust plane, Netflix's Java control plane) or a Kubernetes-native Endpoint Picker bound to an InferencePool (Tesla/llm-d, the GKE/Envoy Gateway Inference Extension family) — which picks a specific backend pod using live telemetry: KV-cache/prefix-affinity hit potential, queue depth, and GPU/batch utilization, with request-level priority (InferenceObjective's Critical/High/BestEffort, where present) breaking ties under contention. The request lands on a warm vLLM replica (or, behind Triton, is translated into a Triton inference request first), which runs it through continuous batching and paged-attention-managed KV cache, generates tokens, and returns them through whichever framing the caller used (OpenAI-compat streaming or gRPC). Metrics from this whole path — engine-internal (`vllm:*`) and gateway-level (`gen_ai.*` OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions, where adopted) — are scraped into Prometheus and, wherever a shared-backend framework like Triton sits in the path, merged through a custom proxy because the framework's native metrics bridge does not surface engine-internal signals by default.

## Deployment-flow account

A model is chosen (an open-weight checkpoint, possibly continually pretrained/post-trained in-house as at Thomson Reuters, or consolidated from a fragmented fleet into one checkpoint as at T-Tech) and its weights land in the internal artifact registry/object store. Quantization, where used, happens at this stage and before serving, not at runtime: FP8 via `llm-compressor` is the documented path at both Thomson Reuters and (implicitly, "single-GPU FP8 replicas") T-Tech, with sensitive layers (`lm_head`, MoE gate/router weights) deliberately excluded from quantization to protect output quality. A declarative resource (a Helm chart, a Crossplane `InferenceService` claim, or a KServe `LLMInferenceService`/`LLMInferenceConfig` object) is applied through GitOps (ArgoCD or Flux reconciling from a Git-tracked manifest) rather than applied imperatively — every enterprise account that describes its deployment mechanism independently lands on GitOps. This declarative apply triggers, in order: a weight-preloading step (an idempotent job that pulls weights onto shared/fast storage exactly once per model+revision, observed explicitly in the practitioner stack and implicit in Thomson Reuters' and Netflix's pre-materialization strategies); the actual engine pod spec, parameterized per model (tensor-parallel size, context window, dtype, tool-call parser); a KEDA `ScaledObject` wired to that model's own Prometheus metrics; and a gateway route binding the model name to the backend pool. Karpenter (or an equivalent cluster autoscaler) provisions GPU-capable nodes underneath this on a roughly one-minute cycle when no spare capacity exists. Rollout then follows one of two patterns depending on whether the new version's I/O schema is compatible with the old one: a Red-Black traffic shift (new version scales up while old scales down, atomic rollback on health-check failure) for compatible changes, or a fully-independent Versioned deployment (both versions live simultaneously, consumers migrate on their own schedule, old version drained after inactivity) for breaking changes — Netflix's explicit recommendation is to design for the cheaper Red-Black path by making the model spec itself version-agnostic wherever possible. A quality gate (CapTrack/Scales++ at Thomson Reuters; Promptfoo as a nightly CronJob in the practitioner stack) runs against the new deployment, with results fed into the same observability stack as infrastructure metrics, before or alongside the traffic shift — none of the sources describe this gate as strictly blocking the rollout mechanism itself, which is an audited absence worth flagging rather than inferring a hard gate that isn't documented.

## Well-evidenced versus extrapolated

**Well-evidenced across multiple independent deployments** (treat as load-bearing): vLLM's dominance as the engine where no pre-existing multi-framework platform exists; Kubernetes as the orchestration substrate; the gateway/control-plane layer being bespoke in every account without exception; KEDA-on-engine-metrics (not CPU/GPU core %) as the autoscaling signal; GitOps as the deployment mechanism; KV-cache/prefix-affinity-aware routing as a real, measured performance lever (Tesla's 3x/2x numbers are the only concrete before/after in the corpus, but the *direction* — cache-aware beats round-robin — is corroborated structurally by Mooncake, LMCache, and the Gateway API Inference Extension's own design rationale per the consensus claims); FP8-via-llm-compressor as the routine quantization path with sensitive-layer exclusions; the two-family split of cold-start mitigation (streaming vs. snapshot/restore) as the right taxonomy, confirmed against three independent primary sources (CoreWeave, NVIDIA, Modal) each stating its own rationale and numbers.

**Extrapolated from a single deployment, or genuinely thin** (flag before leaning on it): Tesla's specific failure-mode narrative and 3x/2x numbers are one deployment's account, reported by the llm-d project, not independently reproduced elsewhere at that precision — the *direction* generalizes (corroborated by CoreWeave's and the practitioner stack's independent reasons for avoiding the same NFS/EBS pattern), but the exact multipliers should not be quoted as a general expectation. Netflix's specific boot-sequence mechanics (FSx pre-materialization timing, the embedded-vs-standalone Triton split) are single-sourced. T-Tech's replica counts (16-48 pods) and request-rate figures are one organization's operating point, not a benchmark of what vLLM "needs." The "five-phase cold-start" taxonomy and its per-phase timing ranges come from one secondary gray-literature source (llms.blog) whose numbers I could not verify against a primary benchmark — the phase *structure* is plausible and consistent with what Thomson Reuters' and Modal's primary accounts independently imply, but its specific storage-tier throughput table should not be cited as measured fact. The claim that a quality-eval gate blocks rollout (rather than running alongside it) is not stated anywhere in the corpus and should not be inferred. Whether the Gateway API Inference Extension / EPP pattern becomes the converging standard routing mechanism, versus remaining one of several bespoke approaches, is genuinely open — the mechanism's own documentation states it is Alpha and not production-recommended, even though three of the deployments surveyed here (Tesla, the practitioner stack, and implicitly Atlassian's Istio-based routing) are already building on exactly this class of signal-driven routing.

## Committed position

The internal-serving stack has a real, repeatable reference architecture, but it has two load-bearing forks, not zero: an engine-layer fork between vLLM-direct and Triton-fronted-vLLM/TensorRT-LLM that tracks whether the organization already had a non-LLM ML-serving platform to fold LLMs into, and a weight-loading fork between streaming and snapshot/restore that tracks whether the organization's pain point is scale-to-zero latency (snapshot/restore wins, per Modal's own 45s→5s) or steady-state replica-restart risk (pinned-memory-plus-faster-loader wins, per Thomson Reuters' 20min→~5min). Above the engine, the architecture converges hard: Kubernetes, a bespoke gateway/control plane, KEDA-on-engine-metrics autoscaling, GitOps deployment, and cache-aware routing on live backend telemetry are present in every first-person account read for this locus, with no counterexample. The weakest part of this account is the routing-signal layer's maturity: the mechanism (KV-cache/prefix/queue-depth-aware endpoint selection) is unanimous in direction, but the specific implementation converging on it — the Gateway API Inference Extension / EPP — is self-described as Alpha, not production-recommended, and the corpus's own contradiction graph documents its reference implementation's governance as contested. That means the report should describe the *routing-signal pattern* as settled and the *Gateway API Inference Extension as an implementation* as still forming, not treat the two as the same claim.

**Position:** the reference architecture for internal LLM serving is: Kubernetes + a bespoke gateway/control plane + vLLM (or Triton-fronted vLLM/TensorRT-LLM where a pre-existing ML platform exists) + KEDA-on-engine-metrics autoscaling + GitOps-driven declarative deployment + cache-aware endpoint routing, with weight-loading optimized by streaming for scale-to-zero topologies and by pinned-memory/faster-loaders for steady-state fleets — and the two forks named above (engine-fronting, weight-loading strategy) are organizational-history-driven, not technology-quality-driven.

**Confidence:** high for the layer list and the engine/orchestration/gateway convergence (6 independent first-person accounts with zero counterexample); medium for the specific weight-loading numbers and for the claim that the two forks are driven by organizational history rather than something else not visible in these accounts (plausible reading of the evidence, but only 2-3 data points per fork).

**Boundary conditions:** this architecture describes general-purpose internal chat/agentic/retrieval serving at organizations with an existing Kubernetes investment. It does not describe edge/multi-tenant serving under untrusted-workload constraints (Cloudflare's custom Rust engine is the corpus's own named exception, for a stated reason: gvisor sandboxing requirements and a Rust-everywhere standard) or frontier-scale disaggregated serving (DeepSeek, Moonshot/Mooncake build custom schedulers and transport layers because, in their own stated words, "the open-source version of vLLM only supports local KVCache caching" at the scale they operate).

**What would change this position:** a first-person account from an organization running vLLM-direct-on-Kubernetes that does NOT describe a bespoke gateway layer, or that uses plain round-robin/connection-count routing successfully at meaningful scale, would undercut the "gateway is always bespoke" / "cache-aware routing always wins" convergence claims. A primary source showing the Gateway API Inference Extension in GA production use at an organization not already named here (i.e., beyond Tesla) would strengthen the claim that EPP is converging into the standard, rather than remaining one bespoke approach among several. A primary benchmark independently reproducing the llms.blog storage-tier throughput table would let that table be cited as fact rather than flagged as unverified.

**Evidence weight:** 6 independent first-person deployment accounts (Thomson Reuters, T-Tech, Tesla/llm-d, Netflix, Atlassian ×2) support the layer-by-layer convergence claims directly; 1 practitioner GitOps account (Ogenki) corroborates structurally without being an enterprise deployment; 4 primary engineering-blog/repo sources (CoreWeave, NVIDIA, Modal, vLLM Production Stack docs) support the weight-loading/cold-start claims, with 2 of those primary sources directly used to disconfirm fabricated figures found in secondary literature during the width sweep; 1 secondary gray-literature source (llms.blog) supplies the five-phase taxonomy's structure but its specific numbers are unverified and flagged as such; 1 independent technical explainer (jimmysong.io) documents the EPP mechanism's design and its own stated Alpha status.

## Open questions

- No source in this locus describes whether the quality/eval gate (CapTrack/Scales++, Promptfoo) is a hard blocking gate on rollout or an advisory/post-hoc signal — this is an audited absence worth another pass if a draft needs to make a prescriptive claim about eval-gating rollout.
- The organizational-history explanation for the Triton-vs-vLLM-direct fork (pre-existing ML platform vs. greenfield) is my own synthesis across Netflix/Atlassian vs. Thomson Reuters/T-Tech; it was not stated as a general rule by any single source, and a direct account from a third Triton-fronted or third greenfield-vLLM deployment would test it.
- No primary benchmark in the corpus directly compares streaming-loader and snapshot/restore techniques on the same model/hardware — the Modal (0.5B) and NVIDIA/Tensorizer (8-20B) numbers are not commensurable, and a head-to-head would sharpen the "which technique for which topology" guidance considerably.
- The llms.blog storage-tier throughput table (EFS/S3/NVMe/pinned-memory) was not independently verified; worth a targeted fetch of a vendor-neutral benchmark if that exact table needs to appear in the final report as a hard figure rather than illustrative framing.
