# Interim report: managed-middle-hosted-open-weight

**Locus question:** How common and how consequential is the third pattern — running a chosen (often fine-tuned) open-weight model on a managed provider rather than self-hosting it?
**Flavor:** convergent

## What the corpus already said

The width sweep had already surfaced the raw material for this locus but had not assembled it into its own pattern. The consensus claim `hybrid-routing-is-the-norm` cites Harvey and Amplitude as instances of a "middle pattern" nested inside the broader finding that internal platforms route across self-hosted and frontier-API models through one interface — but it treats the middle pattern as a footnote to routing, not as a deployment-location question in its own right [[harvey-tenet-research-preview]]. The contradiction graph's `breakeven-threshold` and `oss-control-plane-sufficiency` clusters establish the self-hosted-vs-API economics debate and the build-vs-adopt boundary, but neither addresses the specific question of who operates the serving stack when an organization has already committed to a *specific, customized* open-weight model. Thomson Reuters' own technical report [[thomson-continual-learning-of-frontier-models-for]] was already in the corpus as the self-hosted end state this locus is implicitly measured against.

## What the new sources say

**1. Fireworks AI's own blog on the Harvey/Kimi K3 collaboration** [[post-training-kimi-k3-with-harvey-for-long-horizon-legal-work]] (published 8/26/2026, five days after Harvey's own post). This is the single most load-bearing new source for this locus. It gives the vendor's side of the Harvey Tenet story and contains the strongest available evidence on where the resulting model actually serves: *"We build the training and inference stacks together, and as a result, training and serving share the same numerics. The checkpoint you post-trained is the one that runs... Because training and serving live on the same platform, a promising checkpoint can be promoted and validated while training continues, then moved into production without leaving the platform."* This is not phrased as a direct confirmation that Harvey's live customer traffic runs on Fireworks — but it is an explicit architectural claim about the product Harvey used, made by the vendor operating it, with a stated technical rationale (train-inference numeric divergence causes token clipping and reward collapse in long RL runs, so keeping serving on the same numerics as training is a stability requirement, not a convenience). The post closes with calls to action — "Train your own model on Fireworks," "Frontier-lab training infrastructure as a service" — confirming this architecture is the product Fireworks is selling, not an incidental detail.

**2. Harvey's own earlier post on post-training with Baseten Research** [[post-training-open-legal-agents-with-baseten-research]] (5/27/2026, three months before the Tenet/Kimi K3 announcement, a different model and a different managed-provider research partner). Harvey post-trained a 27B open-weight model (Qwen3.5-27B) with Baseten Research using iterative SFT plus Baseten's own KV-cache compaction technique ("STILL"). This source cuts against an over-clean reading of the pattern in two ways. First, it is explicitly framed as unfinished research — "A 27B open-weight model landing in the closed-source frontier on LAB is base camp... It is a much bigger lift than this one post, more to come" — with no claim of production deployment at all. Second, in its rationale section it states that post-training's governance benefit is that "open-weight models can be hosted within a firm's own secure cloud environment" — a nod toward self-hosting as the *available option*, not a description of what Harvey actually did with this particular model. Harvey's own silence on serving location, documented in the loci brief for the Tenet/Kimi-K3 case, repeats here for the Baseten/Qwen3.5-27B case.

**3. Baseten's customer case study on Wispr Flow** [[wispr-flow-creates-effortless-voice-dictation-with-llama-on-baseten]]. This is the cleanest confirmed instance of the full pattern anywhere in the corpus: Wispr Flow (voice-dictation product "Flow") chose Meta's open-weight Llama, fine-tuned it for real-time transcript cleanup, and runs it in production on Baseten's dedicated deployments (on AWS workload planes), not on self-managed infrastructure. Unlike the Fireworks/Harvey source, this one states production serving directly rather than architecturally: *"Baseten powers Flow with low-latency inference on dedicated deployments for these fine-tuned Llama models."* Co-founder/CTO Sahaj Garg is quoted giving an explicit rationale that is about engineering leverage, not just cost: *"With Baseten, we gained a lot of control over our entire inference pipeline and worked with Baseten's team to optimize each step,"* and *"We no longer have to reserve GPUs just in case we see usage spikes during viral moments."* The measured production numbers (p99 end-to-end latency under 700ms, the Llama step generating 100+ tokens in under 250ms, SOC 2/HIPAA-compliant regional deployment) are concrete and dated to an active product, not a research preview. This source is vendor-sourced (a Baseten case study page) and therefore sits lower in the evidence hierarchy than a company's own blog, but the quotes are attributed to a named executive and the technical detail (TensorRT-LLM engine builder, Chains multi-step framework, autoscale-to-zero) is specific enough to treat as reliable description of what Baseten's platform did, if read cautiously on what Wispr Flow's own motivations were.

A fourth data point, already in the vault but reread for contrast: **T-Tech** [[from-production-traffic-to-post-training-building-a-self-hosted-llm]] fine-tunes Qwen3-32B and self-hosts it, for an explicitly stated reason — *"Data-residency constraints force enterprises to self-host LLMs"* — that none of the three managed-middle cases state as their own motivation.

## Evidence synthesis

**How many distinct named organizations actually sit in this middle, applying a rigorous test.** The test should require two things together: (a) the organization chose and meaningfully customized an open-weight model (not just called an API that happens to serve one), and (b) a named provider, not the organization itself, operates the serving infrastructure for the resulting model. Applying that test:

- **Harvey + Fireworks (Tenet/Kimi K3):** customization is unambiguous (asynchronous RL/GSPO post-training producing a model that beats the Kimi K3 base by roughly 9 points all-pass on LAB). Managed serving is *architecturally implied but not declaratively confirmed* — Fireworks states its platform is built so a trained checkpoint moves to production "without leaving the platform," which is a claim about what the product does, not a claim that Harvey's customer traffic specifically runs there today.
- **Harvey + Baseten (Qwen3.5-27B):** customization is real (iSFT plus a novel KV-cache compaction technique) but the source frames this explicitly as *research*, not a shipped deployment — this case does not clear the bar for "running" a customized model in production at all, managed or otherwise.
- **Amplitude + Fireworks (Kimi K2.7):** managed serving is confirmed directly ("Fireworks offers a managed catalog... dedicated GPU instances"). Customization fails the test — Amplitude explicitly ran Kimi K2.7 "without post-training the model"; all of its engineering investment went into the surrounding application (routing, caching, typed contracts, failure remediation), not the weights. This is a managed-but-uncustomized case, a different cell in the pattern than Harvey's.
- **Wispr Flow + Baseten (Llama):** both halves confirmed directly and in production — fine-tuned weights, dedicated managed serving on AWS, measured p99 latency on live traffic.
- **Thomson Reuters:** the counter-case. TR customizes aggressively (continual learning on Qwen checkpoints, ~$40M total program cost, a team "not exceeding three dozen engineers and scientists") *and* self-hosts serving (vLLM on Kubernetes across 11 clusters, 4 geographies, 2 cloud providers), reporting a 19.7% cost saving on GLM-5.2 versus third-party inference. TR's own report explicitly argues against the managed alternative: *"managed vendor offerings arguably reduce total cost of ownership by streamlining complex engineering workflows... [but] this alternative to self-hosting introduces a material long-term risk of vendor lock-in, leaving organisations vulnerable to rising costs without a clear exit strategy to alternative infrastructure."*

So the corpus supports **two confirmed instances of the full pattern** (Harvey/Fireworks on the "customized" side is strong-but-inferential; Wispr Flow/Baseten is fully confirmed), **one confirmed instance of the weaker managed-but-uncustomized variant** (Amplitude), **one explicit research-stage, not-yet-shipped attempt** (Harvey/Baseten), and **one deliberate counter-case** (Thomson Reuters) that argues against the pattern by name. The Baseten case-study page also names roughly ten further customers (EliseAI, Gamma, Hebbia, Notion, Oxen AI, OpenEvidence, Parallel, Patreon, Speechify) whose case-study titles suggest several more instances of "trains specialized models" or "fine-tuned model in production," but this report did not fetch or verify those individually — they are reported here as a vendor-curated signal of breadth, not as additional confirmed cases, and should not be counted toward the N.

**What the middle buys and costs.** The clearest statement of cost is TR's own, quoted above: vendor lock-in and exposure to future price increases with no stated exit path. The clearest statement of what it buys is Wispr Flow's: Baseten supplied TensorRT-LLM optimization, a multi-step orchestration framework (Chains), SOC 2/HIPAA certification, and scale-to-zero autoscaling — categories of engineering investment a small voice-app team would otherwise have had to build itself to hit a sub-700ms p99 target. Amplitude's account adds a narrower, more mechanical framing of the tradeoff: choosing Fireworks's managed catalog over Modal's "raw GPU containers where you bring your own serving stack and own every part of quantization and batching yourself" meant giving up control over quantization and batching specifically, in exchange for "no infrastructure to operate." None of the three managed-middle sources describe this tradeoff as costly in practice; all three describe it as the obviously correct choice given what the organization was optimizing for. Only TR, which did not choose the managed middle, supplies the argument for why it might be costly — and TR's argument is about an organization at a different scale and with a different motivating constraint (data/model sovereignty for a 170-year-old institution operating across jurisdictions) than any of the three managed-middle companies.

**Transitional or stable.** This is the locus's central question, and the new evidence shifts the answer from "unclear, built on silence" to "most likely stable, for a specific and statable reason." The Fireworks post is not describing a stopgap; it is describing a designed technical dependency. Training-serving numeric alignment is not an onboarding convenience that erodes once a customer "graduates" to self-hosting — it is, by Fireworks's own account, the mechanism that keeps a long asynchronous RL run from collapsing (train-inference KL divergence causes token clipping and reward collapse past a threshold). An organization that wants to keep *improving* a post-trained model over time via continued RL has a structural reason to keep training and serving on the same platform, not merely a cost or convenience reason to start there. This argues for stability specifically in the post-training-via-RL sub-case (Harvey/Tenet), more than in the no-customization sub-case (Amplitude), where the managed choice is a more ordinary buy-vs-build decision that could in principle be reversed without touching any model asset. The counter-evidence is Thomson Reuters, but TR is not evidence of a *transition* — nothing in TR's own report describes TR having previously used a managed provider and exited; TR states sovereignty as a from-the-outset design principle, backed by an approximately $40M program and a team of up to three dozen engineers and scientists. TR is evidence that full self-hosting is a real, available end state — not evidence that the managed middle is a waypoint most organizations pass through en route to it. The cost of TR's end state is specific and large enough that it looks more like a deliberate strategic bet available to a narrow set of organizations than a default trajectory.

## Committed position

The managed middle is the more likely stable resting point for organizations that customize or choose a specific open-weight model but whose core differentiation is a product or application layer rather than infrastructure — and it is structurally reinforced, not merely tolerated, by the training-as-a-service vendors themselves, because keeping training and serving numerically aligned on one platform is what makes iterative post-training (especially RL) stable in the first place. Full self-hosting of both training and serving, as Thomson Reuters does, is a real and available alternative but is evidenced in this corpus as a deliberate, expensive, sovereignty-motivated choice (~$40M, up to three dozen engineers) rather than as the terminus of a path that starts in the managed middle. The report should therefore present "alternatives to frontier models" as organized along two independent axes — self-host vs. managed, and customize weights vs. don't — rather than as a single self-hosted-vs-API spectrum; the managed-and-customized quadrant is populated by confirmed named cases (Wispr Flow/Baseten; Harvey/Fireworks with the caveat below) and is being actively built and sold as a durable architecture, not a temporary bridge, by at least two infrastructure vendors.

- **Position:** The managed middle (choose/customize an open-weight model, let a provider operate serving) is a stable architecture for product-focused organizations doing active post-training, reinforced by platform design (train-serve numeric alignment) rather than being a transitional stage toward self-hosting; full self-hosting is a separate, rarer path taken for sovereignty reasons, not a natural next step from the managed middle.
- **Confidence:** medium. The "stable" claim rests most heavily on one vendor's (Fireworks's) architectural description of its own product, which has a direct commercial interest in customers believing they need not leave — that source should be read as a credible design-intent claim, not an independent, time-tested measurement of customer retention. The "confirmed full pattern" count is genuinely small: one fully confirmed case (Wispr Flow), one strongly-implied-but-not-declared case (Harvey/Tenet), one confirmed managed-but-uncustomized case (Amplitude), and one explicit research-not-production case (Harvey/Baseten).
- **Boundary conditions:** This holds for organizations whose competitive differentiation is a product or application surface (legal AI workflows, product analytics, voice dictation) rather than infrastructure itself, and who either lack the resources or the motivating constraint to build a $40M-scale training-and-serving program. It does not hold, or holds much more weakly, where data residency or model sovereignty is a first-order legal or strategic requirement (Thomson Reuters; also T-Tech, which self-hosts explicitly because "data-residency constraints force enterprises to self-host LLMs") or at request volumes high enough to fund an in-house build — though the corpus separately documents (see `breakeven-threshold` in the contradiction graph) that there is no agreed volume threshold at which that calculus flips, so this boundary is fuzzy rather than sharp.
- **What would change this position:** Direct evidence that Harvey, Amplitude, or Wispr Flow migrated off their managed provider to self-hosted serving within roughly 12–24 months of the coverage read here would flip this from stable to transitional. A published multi-year retention or migration study of fine-tuning-platform customers (Fireworks, Baseten, Together AI) showing that most customers eventually self-host would do the same; no such study exists in the corpus, and I could not find one. Conversely, if Thomson Reuters' own report had described having previously run on a managed provider and exited, that would be evidence that even the most sovereignty-motivated organizations start in the middle — but TR's report frames sovereignty as a founding principle, not a lesson learned from a prior managed stage, so it does not supply that evidence either way.
- **Evidence weight:** 2 cases with confirmed managed serving in production (Wispr Flow fully confirmed; Amplitude confirmed but uncustomized), 1 case with confirmed customization and vendor-architecturally-implied-but-not-declared managed serving (Harvey/Fireworks/Tenet), 1 case explicitly framed as research rather than production (Harvey/Baseten/Qwen3.5-27B), 1 strong counter-case arguing against the pattern from direct experience and at stated cost (Thomson Reuters), plus an unverified vendor-curated list (~10 further Baseten customer names) suggestive of wider prevalence but not independently checked here.

## Open questions

- Does Harvey's live customer-facing Tenet traffic actually run on Fireworks, or did Harvey export the trained checkpoint to its own infrastructure after training completed? No source answers this directly; Fireworks's "without leaving the platform" language is the closest available evidence and is architecturally suggestive, not declarative.
- Of the ~10 further named Baseten customers surfaced on the Wispr Flow case-study page (EliseAI, Gamma, Hebbia, Notion, Oxen AI, OpenEvidence, Parallel, Patreon, Speechify), how many actually fit the strict two-part test (customized weights + managed serving) versus using Baseten only for off-the-shelf model hosting? Unverified in this pass; a follow-up fetch batch of 3-4 of these case studies would materially firm up the prevalence estimate.
- Is there a published case of an organization that started in the managed middle (fine-tuned model, managed serving) and later moved the same model to self-hosted infrastructure for cost or control reasons? This is the single piece of evidence that would most directly test the transitional-vs-stable question, and I did not find one in the time available.
- What does Together AI's own customer base look like for this same pattern? The fetcher's search for a Together AI case study with Wispr-Flow-level specificity came back empty; this may be a gap in search depth rather than a real absence.
